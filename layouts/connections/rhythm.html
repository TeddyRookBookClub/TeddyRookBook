Deep dive · Music ∩ Math · Pi and the primes, by ear and by eye

{{ .Title }}

It started with a simple guess of Teddy Rook’s: π ≈ 3 + k/(7k + 1). Put in k = 16 and you get 355/113, a famous fraction for pi. Then play pi as a beat. You hear a rhythm: gaps of 7 beats, with a gap of 8 about every 16th time, and a rare short phrase after 292 longer ones. Each section below follows that rhythm, or the rule that builds it, to a place where it meets the prime numbers.

The number in the bar at the top drives every instrument. Swap pi for another number to see what makes pi special. Each section has a green “Pi ↔ primes” line that names the link.

Chapter I

Pi’s machine

The continued fraction: the rule that builds pi’s rhythm, and the rule every later section reuses.

1 · The starting guess

3 + k/(7k + 1) finds pi at k ≈ 16

The fraction $\frac{k}{7k+1}$ is the same as $\cfrac{1}{7+\frac{1}{k}}$. Set it equal to π − 3 and solve, and you get k = 15.9966. Round that to 16 and you get 355/113, which matches pi to six decimal places. The Chinese mathematician Zu Chongzhi found the same fraction about AD 480; it is called Milü, “the close ratio.” Put in k = 10 instead and you get 223/71, the lower bound Archimedes proved.

Pi ↔ primesThe denominators 7, 71 and 113 of pi’s three classic fractions are all prime. Amber dots mark every k where 7k + 1 is prime. Section 4 tests whether pi favors primes or whether this is luck.

More ways to find πTwo surprising roads to pi that have nothing to do with fractions. Colliding blocks: slide a heavy block at a light one by a wall and count the clacks, 3, 31, 314, 3141, which spell out pi’s decimal digits through an angle. Dropped sticks: the share of sticks that cross the lines on a floor estimates pi by chance.

2 · The levels

The 16 was the next level down

1/(π − 3) = 7.0625, so the gaps between hits are 7 or 8 beats. The leftover 0.0625 is 1/15.9966, which is the k from the guess. It says an 8 comes about once every 16 gaps. Go one level deeper and 15.9966 = 15 + 1/(1 + 1/292.6). Every term of pi’s continued fraction, [7; 15, 1, 292, …], is a repeat count in the rhythm. The table follows the number in the bar.

LevelTermBlock (beats)HitsFractionBlock length prime?What you hear

$$\role[next block]{output}{q_{n+1}} = \role[repeat count, the term]{coefficient}{a_{n+1}}\cdot \role[this block]{base}{q_n} + \role[previous block]{index}{q_{n-1}}$$

In words: next block = (term × this block) + previous block. It is the textbook rule for continued-fraction denominators, read here as a rule for musical form.

rhythm(terms a1, a2, a3, …):      # any number between 0 and 1
  prev = "x"                        # one hit
  this = "."                        # one rest
  this, prev = "."×(a1 − 1) + "x", this
  for each next term a:
      this, prev = this×a + prev, this
  return this                       # x = hit, . = rest

For pi, the level-4 block is 292 phrases of 113 beats followed by one short phrase of 106. Counting the first beat as beat 0, the short phrase starts at beat 32,996 (that is, 292 × 113) and ends the block at beat 33,102. Checked by computer over 140,000 beats: in the first 80,000 beats there are 706 whole phrases of 113 and 2 of 106.

Mathematicians call these Sturmian or Christoffel words. They are the most evenly spread rhythms possible, which is why Euclid’s algorithm and Euclidean rhythms (section 6) produce them too. What this page adds is reading the continued fraction as musical form: bar, phrase, section, movement.

Pi ↔ primesEach block holds a whole number of hits, and a block’s length and its number of hits never share a factor (because p·q′ − p′·q = ±1 for neighboring fractions). So every level of pi’s rhythm is a pair of coprime numbers, which is what Chapter III builds on.

4 · The ladder

Are pi’s best fractions unusually prime?

Stop the continued fraction after each term and you get the number’s best fractions, called convergents. For pi they are 3, 22/7, 333/106, 355/113, 103993/33102, …. A large term means the fraction before it was unusually good, which is why the 292 makes 355/113 so accurate. Prime numerators and denominators are marked ◆. Switch the bar’s number to compare pi with other constants.

#NumeratorDenominatorError

Pi ↔ primesAmong pi’s first 400 convergents, 4 denominators are prime: 7, 113, 265,381 and an 18-digit one. A fair estimate uses the odds of a random number that size (about 1 in ln q), raised a little because a convergent’s denominator never shares a factor with its numerator. That predicts about 7.4. Over the first 5,000 convergents the count is 9 prime denominators against about 10.9 expected (see the paper), and over the first 50,000 it is 14 against about 14.1 (the last four, at 25,332, 27,651, 32,394 and 37,721, are probable primes found by Teddy Rook in November 2026, along with a new prime numerator at 42,037: five new probable primes in all). So pi looks like a typical number here: its early primes are luck, not a law. Whether pi has infinitely many prime denominators is an open question (what we found).

Chapter II

Pi and primes in sound

Tuning, polyrhythm, clocks that fall into step, and the primes as a melody.

5 · The circle of fifths

12 notes come from the same ladder as 22/7

A pure fifth is the frequency ratio 3:2, which is log₂(3/2) = 0.58496 of an octave. Its best fractions run 1/2, 3/5, 7/12, 24/41, 31/53. “A fifth is 7 of 12 half steps” is the 7/12 fraction, found by the same rule that finds 22/7 for pi. The 12-note fifth is 1.96 cents flat, and over 12 fifths that small miss adds up to the Pythagorean comma, 23.46 cents. The next good stops are 41 and 53 notes per octave, and 53 is prime.

Pi ↔ primesStepping around an n-note circle by a step of s visits every note only if s and n share no factor. In a tuning with a prime number of notes (7, 31, 53), every step visits every note. The readout counts the steps that do: Euler’s φ(n).

6 · Euclid’s algorithm

One algorithm builds pi’s ladder, finds shared factors, and spreads beats

Euclid’s algorithm divides and keeps the remainder, again and again. Run it on 355 and 113 and the quotients are 3, 7, 16: pi’s continued fraction, cut off. It ends at the greatest common divisor, the oldest test for whether two numbers share a prime. In 2003 the physicist Eric Bjorklund used the same steps to spread pulses evenly in a particle accelerator, and in 2005 Godfried Toussaint showed that the results are rhythms found in music around the world.

Pi ↔ primesTry “Pi: 16 in 113.” Euclid’s quotients come out as pi’s continued fraction, and the rhythm you hear is the third level of pi’s beat. With a prime number of slots, every pulse count gives a rhythm that never repeats inside the bar.

7 · Mode locking

Clocks lock to fractions, and there are about 3N²/π² of them

Things that swing or spin and nudge each other fall into step at simple ratios. Christiaan Huygens saw two pendulum clocks on one beam fall into step in 1665. Mercury spins exactly 3 times for every 2 trips around the Sun. Heart cells follow the beat of a pacemaker. A simple model, the circle map, shows all of these: as the coupling K grows, “tongues” open at each fraction p/q, and inside a tongue the motion snaps to that fraction.

Pi ↔ primesEach tongue is a fraction in lowest terms, so p and q share no prime. Counting them up to denominator N gives about 3N²/π², the coprime chance 6/π² again. Pi’s own rotation, 0.14159…, is irrational, so with no coupling it never locks. Raise K and it is dragged down, until the 0/1 tongue captures it at K ≈ 0.89.

8 · Prime music

Primes as melody, prime gaps as rhythm

Number the 12 notes of the octave 0 to 11, starting at C. Every prime above 3 leaves a remainder of 1, 5, 7 or 11 when divided by 12, so it lands on one of four notes: C♯, F, G or B. Play the primes in order and let each gap to the next prime set the note’s length. In 2016 Robert Lemke Oliver and Kannan Soundararajan found that a prime tends not to repeat the remainder of the prime before it. With no memory, repeats would be 25% of pairs. Among the primes up to a million they are 15%, and up to ten million 16.4%. You hear it as fewer repeated notes than chance would give.

Pi ↔ primesThe four notes are the remainders that share no factor with 12, and they are φ(12)/12 = 1/3 of the octave. Average that share, φ(n)/n, over every n and the answer is 6/π², the orchard’s number in section 9. Change the count below and the primes use a different share of the notes, but the average is always set by pi.

Chapter III

Where pi is made of primes

6/π², π²/6, and the patterns primes leave on pi’s own spirals.

9 · The orchard

Two random whole numbers share no prime 6/π² of the time

Stand at the corner of an orchard planted on a square grid. The trees you can see, with no tree in front of them, are the points (a, b) where a and b share no factor. They fill 6/π² ≈ 60.79% of the grid.

Pi ↔ primesThe chance that a prime p divides both a and b is 1/p², so the chance that no prime divides both is the product of (1 − 1/p²) over every prime. Euler proved that $\prod_p \left(1-\frac{1}{p^2}\right) = \frac{6}{\pi^2}$. Every convergent of pi, such as (113, 16), is one of the visible trees.

10 · The correlation matrix

Coprime means uncorrelated

The orchard is more than a picture that looks like a correlation matrix. It is one. Treat “a hit every a-th beat” as a signal, and give every a from 2 to N its own pulse train. Two pulse trains a and b line up once every lcm(a, b) beats, and their correlation works out to exactly

$$\operatorname{corr}(a,b) = \frac{\role[shared factor]{coefficient}{\gcd(a,b)} - 1}{\sqrt{(a-1)(b-1)}}$$

On the diagonal the gcd is a itself, so the correlation is 1. Off the diagonal the correlation is zero exactly when a and b are coprime. So the zero cells of this matrix are the orchard’s visible trees, and they fill 6/π² of it.

Pi ↔ primesA prime’s row is zero except at its own multiples, so the primes are the “independent” pulse trains of arithmetic. The share of uncorrelated pairs tends to 6/π². In music this is polyrhythm: 3 against 4 meet only on the downbeat (correlation 0), while 4 against 6 keep colliding (correlation 0.26).

11 · Chemistry: hydrogen

Hydrogen’s 1/n² adds up to π²/6, a product over primes

Hydrogen’s energy levels go as −1/n², and its visible lines, the Balmer series, come from the differences 1/4 − 1/n². In wavelengths that is λ = 364.56 · n²/(n² − 4) nanometers, the formula Johann Balmer found in 1885. Electron shells hold 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.

Pi ↔ primesAdd up hydrogen’s squares, 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + …, and you get π²/6. Euler showed this equals $\frac{1}{1-1/4}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/9}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/25}\cdots$, with one factor for each prime. It is the orchard’s 6/π² turned upside down. The match is in the formulas: hydrogen doesn’t know about primes, but its 1/n² is the same series.

12 · Pi’s sunflower, seeded with primes

Primes avoid the arms that share a factor

Place seed number k at k × (the bar’s number) turns around a circle, at a distance √k from the center. For π − 3 the seeds fall into 7 spokes that curl into 113 arms, because those are pi’s block lengths. Now light up only the seeds whose numbers are prime.

Pi ↔ primesArm j of q arms holds the seeds k that leave remainder j when divided by q. Primes stay out of every arm whose j shares a factor with q, and fill the rest equally, as Dirichlet proved in 1837. Pi’s 7 and 113 are prime, so primes avoid just one of the 7 spokes and one of the 113 arms. Try the 106 arms (2 × 53) with more seeds, and half the arms go dark.

13 · Sieves and hidden periods

Wrap the line, and hidden periods stand up

The sunflower, straightened out. Lay the numbers in rows of a chosen width. When the width matches a hidden period, the hits line up in straight columns. When it is slightly off, the columns lean, and the lean shows the error. Pi’s rhythm stands up at its block lengths 7, 106 and 113. The primes line up in the columns that share no factor with the width.

Pi ↔ primesAt width 113, pi’s rhythm forms 16 nearly straight columns, and the primes fill 112 columns, all but the zero column, because 113 is prime. At width 30, primes use only 8 of the 30 columns. Prime sieves use this trick, called wheel factorization, to skip 73% of numbers without checking them.

Chapter IV

Primes in matter

X-ray diffraction and nuclear physics both treat pi’s rhythm and the primes as the same kind of object.

14 · Chemistry: quasicrystals

Pi’s rhythm and the primes both diffract like crystals

In 1982 Dan Shechtman found a crystal that was ordered but never repeated, a quasicrystal. Many scientists doubted it at first; it won him the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Crystallographers read order from diffraction, which is the Fourier transform of where the atoms sit. Pi’s rhythm is a one-dimensional quasicrystal: its diffraction has sharp spikes at multiples of π − 3, wrapped around 1.

Pi ↔ primesPut atoms at the primes and diffract them, and you also get sharp spikes, at fractions with square-free denominators: 1/2, 1/3, 1/6, 1/5, but never 1/4 or 1/9. Salvatore Torquato and colleagues reported this in 2018 and called the primes “effectively limit-periodic.” Freeman Dyson had suggested that classifying one-dimensional quasicrystals might lead to a proof of the Riemann hypothesis.

15 · Nuclear physics: zeta zeros

The primes are a sum of waves, tuned like heavy nuclei

Bernhard Riemann’s explicit formula rebuilds the prime staircase, which climbs by log p at every prime power, from waves whose frequencies are the zeros of the zeta function: 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, …. Add more zeros and the curve snaps to the steps. In 1972 Hugh Montgomery and Freeman Dyson noticed that the spacings between the zeros follow the same statistics as the energy levels of heavy atomic nuclei. No one yet knows why.

Pi ↔ primesThe formula is $\psi(x) = x - \sum_\rho \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \log 2\pi - \tfrac12\log\left(1-x^{-2}\right)$, so pi sits inside the formula that turns waves into primes. The zeta function at 2 is π²/6, the hydrogen sum of section 11. This is the sound version: the primes as a chord.

16 · Factoring

The machine that finds 355/113 also splits numbers into primes

In 1975 Michael Morrison and John Brillhart published how they used continued fractions to factor 2¹²⁸ + 1. Run the continued fraction of √N, the same steps as pi’s ladder. Each step gives a small number Q that matches a perfect square, counting remainders after dividing by N. When Q is itself a perfect square, one gcd splits N. This page runs the simple version that D. H. Lehmer and R. E. Powers described in 1931.

Pi ↔ primesThis is the clearest case of pi’s machine doing prime work. Run on π, it gives 22/7 and 355/113. Run on √N, it finds N’s prime factors. Try 2³² + 1, which Euler showed in 1732 is not prime.

17 · Mersenne circles

Doubling on a 2p − 1 clock rotates rhythms

A times-table circle joins each point x to 2x. Use 2p − 1 points and doubling does something exact: write x in binary with p digits, and doubling just rotates the digits one place. So each orbit is one p-beat rhythm and all of its rotations, a necklace. When p is prime, every orbit has exactly p members, so p divides 2p − 2. That is Fermat’s little theorem, proved with rhythms. It does not make 2p − 1 prime, though: try p = 11, where 2047 = 23 × 89. The real test is the Lucas–Lehmer test. The largest prime known, 2136,279,841 − 1, with 41,024,320 digits, was found with it in October 2024 by the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search.

Pi ↔ primesEvery orbit here is a rhythm, as pi’s beat is. The all-hit rhythms 2p − 1 with prime p are prime far more often than random numbers of their size, which is why the record primes are Mersenne primes.

19 · The prime hunt

Does pi’s rhythm attract primes?

Read the first n beats of the bar’s number as one long binary number, and test it for being prime as n grows. Compare with what chance predicts: an odd number with n binary digits is prime about 2 times in n·ln 2.

Pi ↔ primesOver 1,500 beats, π − 3 gave 1 prime (at beat 49) against 2.4 expected. e − 2 gave 14 against 14, √2 − 1 gave 11 against 9, and φ − 1 gave 13 against 12. Pi’s rhythm doesn’t attract primes. A construction that clearly beat its chance count here would be a real discovery; Mersenne numbers passed that kind of test long ago.

20 · The polyrhythm layerer

Stack the levels of a number, one ring each

Each ring is one track: a convergent p/q played as p hits spread evenly over q slots, all rings taking the same time to go around. Stack pi’s 1/7, 15/106 and 16/113 and you hear three versions of the same beat drift and meet. Or layer different constants. Each track has its own voice, a mute and a solo.

21 · The Euclidean drum machine

Four drums, each spread as evenly as possible

Pick how many hits (pulses) and how many slots each drum gets, and the machine spreads the hits as evenly as it can with Bjorklund’s method from section 6. Rotate a track to start it on a different hit. Tracks of different lengths drift against each other and line up again after their least common multiple. Each track shows the continued fraction of slots/pulses and whether the two numbers are coprime.

Pi ↔ primesWhen pulses and slots are coprime, the pattern never repeats inside its own bar. With a prime number of slots that is true for every pulse count, as in pi’s 7 and 113.

22 · Compose a piece

Write a piece from a number, then keep it

This is Teddy Rook’s idea grown into a small composing machine. The number’s rhythm gives the beats. Its levels give the form: the shortest blocks are bars, the next level up gives phrases, and the next gives sections. (A level that only adds one beat is skipped, so for pi the bars are 7 beats, the phrases 113, and the sections 33,102.) Each level gets a voice: melody on the hits, a bass note at each bar, a chord at each phrase, and a cymbal and key change at each section. Choose the number, the scale, the voices and the length, then play it, look at the score, and download it as a MIDI file for any music program, or as a WAV sound file.

Pi ↔ primesFor pi the bars are 7 beats long and the phrases 106 or 113, so the form itself is built from primes: 7 and 113 are prime and 106 is 2 × 53.

A number, then a smaller one, then a smaller one after that

Teddy Rook’s way of picturing pi: a number, then a smaller number after it, then a smaller one after that, on and on. The rhythm sieve: hear where a prime fraction comes from

Take the bottom numbers of pi’s best fractions, one per beat. Give each small prime a voice, and let it sound on the beats where it divides that beat’s bottom number. The 2 sounds on about one beat in three, the 3 on one in four, and so on. No voice ever sounds on two beats in a row, because neighboring bottom numbers share no factor. A beat where every voice is silent has no small factor, and every prime bottom number above 19 is such a beat (a gold star). This is how Teddy Rook’s search for pi’s new prime fractions threw out 19 candidates in 20 before testing a single huge number: the same rhythm, run with every prime below ten million as a voice.

Rows are the voices 2 to 19; a green dot means that prime divides the bottom number on that beat. Shaded columns are silent beats. Keyboard: focus the picture and use the left and right arrows (one beat) or Page Up and Page Down (a screen).

    Threads to pull

    Questions this page raises about pi and the primes. Some are open, some are known, and some are experiments to try. Treat them as starting points, not results. Several of them (prime convergents, tunings with a prime number of notes, the correlation spectrum) are among the six questions we went back to: see Working on the open questions for what was found.

    Open

    Prime convergents

    Does pi’s continued fraction have infinitely many prime denominators? No one knows. The same question for φ is the Fibonacci prime problem, and for √2 it is the Pell primes. Both are unsolved. A good experiment: count prime convergents for many constants and compare each count with chance. What we found →

    Open

    Primes and nuclei

    The Montgomery–Dyson match (section 15) remains one of the strangest links in science. Hearing the zeros’ spacings next to random and nuclear spacings is a good way to sense why mathematicians take it seriously.

    Known

    Rhythm necklaces count primes

    The rhythm proof of Fermat’s little theorem in section 17 is classical. Counting necklaces also gives the number of irreducible polynomials, and the number of different p-beat rhythms up to rotation.

    Constants as composed music

    Each convergent of pi is a Euclidean rhythm, and each refines the last: E(1,7), then E(16,113), then a 33,102-beat cycle. Layer them in the Polyrhythm layerer. The same works for any constant: √2 makes a rhythm built only from 2s, and e’s rhythm has a growing pattern.

    Tunings with a prime number of notes

    7 notes in a scale, the 31-note tuning Christiaan Huygens favored (its major third is within a cent of pure), and 127 are all Mersenne primes. 12, 41 and 53 come from the fifth’s ladder. Rank every tuning up to 200 notes by how well it does the fifth, the third and the seventh at once, then see whether prime sizes stand out. They probably won’t, but a clear “no” is still a result.

    The correlation spectrum

    Find the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix in section 10. They are the arithmetic version of a stock-market correlation spectrum. How far do they sit from random noise, and does the gap involve 6/π²?

    Two diffraction patterns at once

    Diffract the primes and pi’s rhythm on one strip (section 14). The primes peak at fractions with small square-free denominators, and pi peaks near multiples of 1/7. Where do the two patterns line up?

    A golden sunflower of primes

    Set the bar to φ − 1 and seed the sunflower of section 12 with primes. Its arms come in Fibonacci numbers, and primes skip the arms that share a factor with the arm count. The arm counts that are prime (2, 3, 5, 13, 89, 233, …) leave just one arm empty.

    Squares, shells and the zeta sum

    Shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32 electrons; hydrogen’s levels go as 1/n²; and ζ(2) = Σ 1/n² = π²/6 = Π 1/(1 − p⁻²). One picture could show the squares stacked as shells, their reciprocals adding up to π²/6, and the prime product beside them, labeled clearly as a shared formula rather than shared physics.
