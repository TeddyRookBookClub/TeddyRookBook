Does advertising raise sales, or do stores that sell more just advertise more? Does a training program raise wages? Will prices rise next quarter? Econometrics is the craft of answering questions like these with data. This course starts with a cloud of points and a straight line, and climbs, one chapter at a time, to the methods behind several Nobel prizes: instrumental variables, panel data, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, maximum likelihood, time series and modern causal inference. Every chapter opens with a live simulation you can break.

What econometrics is for

Econometrics uses data to put numbers on economic relationships: how much demand falls when a price rises, how much a year of school adds to earnings, how fast inflation responds to interest rates. Its special strength is telling cause from coincidence when you can’t run an experiment, by finding comparisons that work like one. And it is honest about uncertainty: every estimate comes with a measure of how far off it could be.

The chapters

Each chapter starts with an interactive and a list of missions, then teaches the ideas from first principles with worked examples, practice problems with full answers and a short quiz. The course climbs in three stages, from a curious beginner to the level of a first-year graduate course.

The toolkit at a glance

The whole course on one sheet, in the notation of Wooldridge’s Introductory Econometrics: Greek letters are true values in the population, hats mark estimates from a sample, and û is a residual.

β̂₁ = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) ÷ Σ(xi − x̄)²
Ordinary least squares

The line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. With many regressors, β̂ = (X′X)⁻¹X′y.

Chapters 4 and 6
se(β̂₁) = σ̂ ÷ √SSTx
Standard error

How much the slope would wobble from sample to sample; σ̂² = SSR/(n − 2).

Chapter 5
t = (β̂j − βj) ÷ se(β̂j)
t statistic

How many standard errors an estimate is from a hypothesized value; beyond about ±2 is significant at 5%.

Chapter 5
R² = 1 − SSR ÷ SST
R-squared

The share of the variation in y the regression explains. A measure of fit, not of cause.

Chapter 4
E(β̃₁) = β₁ + β₂δ̃₁
Omitted-variable bias

Leave out x₂ and the slope on x₁ absorbs its effect β₂, times how x₂ moves with x₁ (δ̃₁).

Chapter 6
β̂IV = Cov(z, y) ÷ Cov(z, x)
Instrumental variables and 2SLS

Use only the part of x moved by an instrument z that affects y through x alone.

Chapter 10
ÿit = β₁ẍit + üit
Fixed effects

Subtract each unit’s own average over time, so every unit is compared only with itself.

Chapter 11
δ̂ = (ȳT,after − ȳT,before) − (ȳC,after − ȳC,before)
Difference-in-differences

The treated group’s change minus the comparison group’s change, assuming they would have trended alike.

Chapter 12
τ = limx↓c E(y | x) − limx↑c E(y | x)
Regression discontinuity

The jump in outcomes at a cutoff, comparing people just above it with people just below.

Chapter 13
P(y = 1 | x) = exβ ÷ (1 + exβ)
Logit

A model for yes-or-no outcomes that keeps probabilities between 0 and 1, fitted by maximum likelihood.

Chapter 14
yt = ρyt−1 + et, |ρ| < 1
AR(1)

Today depends on yesterday. With |ρ| < 1 shocks fade away; with ρ = 1 they last forever (a unit root).

What to know first

To start: high-school algebra, comfort with a graph, and the summation sign Σ. Chapters 2 and 3 teach the probability and statistics the course needs from the beginning, so you don’t need a statistics class first. A little calculus helps in Chapter 4, where setting a derivative to zero finds the best-fitting line, but each step is shown.

For the later chapters: from Chapter 6 on, regressions with many variables are written with matrices, so some linear algebra pays off, and maximum likelihood in Chapter 14 uses logarithms and derivatives. For a refresher, the Math Ladder has courses on Probability and Statistics and Linear Algebra.

A short history

  1. 1805 and 1809

    Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares for fitting comets’ orbits; Carl Friedrich Gauss published it in 1809, said he had used it since 1795, and tied it to the normal curve of errors.

  2. 1886

    Francis Galton found that tall parents have children who are tall, but less so, and called it regression toward the mean. The method has been called regression ever since (Chapter 4).

  3. 1920s and 1930s

    R. A. Fisher developed maximum likelihood, popularized significance tests, and made random assignment the foundation of experiments in The Design of Experiments (1935). In 1930 Ragnar Frisch and others founded the Econometric Society; its journal, Econometrica, began in 1933.

  4. 1944

    Trygve Haavelmo’s “The Probability Approach in Econometrics” argued that economic data should be treated as draws from probability models, the basis of everything in this course. It earned him the Nobel prize in 1989.

  5. 1969

    The first Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences went to Ragnar Frisch and Jan Tinbergen “for having developed and applied dynamic models for the analysis of economic processes.”

  6. 2000

    James Heckman, “for his development of theory and methods for analyzing selective samples,” and Daniel McFadden, “for his development of theory and methods for analyzing discrete choice” (Chapter 14).

  7. 2003

    Robert Engle, “for methods of analyzing economic time series with time-varying volatility (ARCH),” and Clive Granger, “for methods of analyzing economic time series with common trends (cointegration)” (Chapter 15).

  8. 2011

    Thomas Sargent and Christopher Sims, “for their empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy,” including the vector autoregressions of Chapter 16.

  9. 2021

    David Card, “for his empirical contributions to labour economics,” and Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens, “for their methodological contributions to the analysis of causal relationships”: the natural experiments of Chapters 10 to 13.
