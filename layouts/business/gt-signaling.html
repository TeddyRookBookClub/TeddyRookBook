Game Theory · Chapter 10

{{ .Title }}

Anyone can say “I’m a hard worker” or “our product is the best”. Words are cheap, so they convince no one. What convinces people is an action that would be too expensive for a pretender: years of school, a ten-year warranty, a costly ad. Here you’ll play employer and set how much education the good job requires, then watch twenty job seekers decide whether to go to school. You’ll find when a degree proves something and when it’s wasted, measure how much an advisor with a bias can credibly tell you, and design a menu of tickets that gets customers to sort themselves.

Missions

    What you’re seeing

    The workers. Half of the twenty job seekers (you can change the share) are high-productivity: each would produce $70,000 a year of value for an employer. The rest would produce $40,000. Each worker knows their own type; employers can’t tell. Here, school teaches nothing useful: it doesn’t change anyone’s productivity. Its only job is to send a signal.

    The cost of school. A year of school costs a low type more than a high type: $20,000 against $10,000 at the start. That’s not just tuition. It stands for effort and stress, which are higher for someone who finds the work harder.

    The wages. Employers compete for workers, so they pay what they expect each worker to be worth. You choose how many years the “degree” takes. In separating mode, employers pay $70,000 to anyone with the degree and $40,000 to everyone else. In pooling mode, they expect everyone to have it, so it gets the average wage. In semi-separating mode, the degree’s wage is set so that low types are exactly indifferent, and some of them gamble on it.

    The check. An equilibrium needs every worker to be choosing their best option given the wages, and the wages to match the workers who actually show up in each room. The rooms turn red when employers would be paying the wrong wage.

    Why a costly signal can be believed

    Michael Spence’s 1973 insight: a signal works only if it costs the people who are pretending more than the people who are not. If school is equally easy for everyone, any worker can copy the high types and the degree proves nothing. If it’s harder for low types, there may be an amount of school that high types will put up with but low types won’t.

    Say the productivities are θL and θH, with a gap Δ = θH − θL, and a year of school costs cL or cH. For a separating equilibrium with a degree that takes e* years, two incentive constraints must hold:

    low types don’t copy: θL ≥ θH − cLe*   and   high types still bother: θH − cHe* ≥ θL
    Δ / cL ≤ e* ≤ Δ / cHa separating equilibrium exists exactly when this range is not empty, which needs cL > cH: the signal must be cheaper for the type it identifies

    Economists call the condition cL > cH single crossing: the two types’ indifference lines cross only once, with the low type’s line steeper. On the chart, that’s why a step in the wage schedule can sit above the red dashed line but below the green one.

    Worked example: Spence’s numbers

    θL = $40k, θH = $70k, cL = $20k a year, cH = $10k a year. The gap is Δ = $30k.

    1. Low types stop copying when 30/20 = 1.5 years or more are required.
    2. High types still go when no more than 30/10 = 3 years are required.
    3. So any requirement from 1.5 to 3 years separates. At 2 years: a low type gets $40k without the degree or $70k − $40k = $30k with it, so skips it; a high type gets $70k − $20k = $50k with it, more than $40k, so gets it.

    Notice the waste. School here produces nothing, yet each high type spends $15k to $30k worth of effort just to prove who they are. Everyone would be better off if employers could see productivity directly.

    Perfect Bayesian equilibrium

    Signaling games are dynamic games with incomplete information: Nature picks the worker’s type, the worker chooses education, the employer sees the education (not the type) and sets a wage. The matching solution concept is perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE). It has two parts:

    • Beliefs. After every possible signal, the employer has a belief about the type. When the signal is one that some types send in equilibrium, the belief must follow from Bayes’ rule.
    • Sequential rationality. Every player’s action is a best response at every point, given their beliefs and everyone else’s strategy.

    Bayes’ rule says nothing about signals that nobody sends in equilibrium, such as 0.7 years of school when everyone chooses 0 or 2. Beliefs there are free, and that freedom creates many equilibria. There are three families:

    Separating

    Each type sends a different signal, so the signal reveals the type. Employers learn everything.

    Pooling

    All types send the same signal, which reveals nothing. Here, everyone gets the same education and the average wage.

    Semi-separating

    Some types mix, sending a signal with some probability. Employers learn something but not everything. Also called hybrid or partially pooling.

    Worked example: a semi-separating equilibrium

    Same numbers, half the workers of each type, and a degree that takes 1 year. For low types to be willing to mix, they must be indifferent: the degree must pay θL + cL × 1 = $60k. A wage of $60k means employers believe a degree holder is high-productivity with probability μ, where 70μ + 40(1 − μ) = 60, so μ = 2/3. By Bayes’ rule, μ = 0.5 / (0.5 + 0.5π), where π is the share of low types who go to school. Solving, π = 0.5. Half of the low types get the degree, and the degree holders are two-thirds high types: exactly what employers expect.

    Too many equilibria: the intuitive criterion

    With the starting numbers, separating equilibria exist for every requirement from 1.5 to 3 years, and pooling equilibria for every level from 0 to 0.75 years. Which is reasonable? In 1987 In-Koo Cho and David Kreps proposed a test. Take an equilibrium and an education level nobody chooses. If a low type would lose by choosing it even if employers then treated them as a high type, while a high type would gain if treated as a high type, then employers shouldn’t believe it comes from a low type.

    Apply it to pooling at zero school, where everyone earns the average wage of $55k. A worker who shows up with 1 year of school says, in effect: “a low type would never do this, because even at $70k they’d net $70k − $20k = $50k, less than the $55k they get now. I would, because I’d net $70k − $10k = $60k.” Employers who think it through pay $70k, and the pooling equilibrium falls apart. The same argument kills every separating equilibrium that requires more than 1.5 years. The one that survives is the cheapest separating signal, Δ/cL, often called the Riley outcome after John Riley’s 1979 paper.

    Try this: in separating mode, drag the requirement from 3 years down to 1.5. Every point in the green band is an equilibrium, but the checklist’s last line is green only at 1.5. Then switch to pooling and try any level: the intuitive criterion always finds an education level that breaks it.
    Common mistake: “This proves school is only a signal”

    Spence’s model assumes school teaches nothing, to see what signaling alone can do. Real education both builds skills and signals them, and economists still argue about how much of the payoff to a degree is each. The model’s lesson is narrower and sharper: a credential can be valuable to its holder even if it adds nothing to their productivity, and the cost of earning it is part of what makes it believable.

    Cheap talk: what words can do

    Words cost nothing to say, so can they carry any information? Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel answered in 1982: yes, if the speaker and listener want similar things, and less the more their interests differ. In their model, an advisor knows the best choice θ for you, somewhere from 0 to 100, but would like you to choose a bit more: θ + b. A contractor recommending how big a renovation to do is a familiar example. The advisor can say anything. You know her bias.

    If she could name the exact number, she would exaggerate it, and you would correct for it, so exact talk can’t be an equilibrium. What can be believed is a rough zone. In every equilibrium, she divides the line into zones and tells you which one θ is in; you choose the middle of that zone. The zones grow wider toward the top, because the advisor’s pull to the right has to be offset there.

    zone boundaries: ai = i/N + 2b i(i − N), for i = 0, …, N  ·  possible only when 2b N(N − 1) < 1 (on a 0-to-1 scale)
    Worked example: a bias of 10 points

    With b = 0.1 on a 0-to-1 scale, two zones are possible (2 × 0.1 × 2 × 1 = 0.4 < 1) but not three (2 × 0.1 × 3 × 2 = 1.2). The boundary is a1 = 1/2 + 2 × 0.1 × 1 × (1 − 2) = 0.3. So the advisor says “low” for 0 to 30, and you choose 15, or “high” for 30 to 100, and you choose 65. Check the advisor at θ = 0.3 is indifferent: her favorite is 0.4, which is 0.25 from 0.15 and 0.25 from 0.65. Your average squared miss is (0.3³ + 0.7³)/12 ≈ 0.031, against 1/12 ≈ 0.083 if she said nothing useful. A bias of a quarter of the range or more makes all talk useless.

    Screening: let customers sort themselves

    Signaling is when the informed side moves first. Screening is when the uninformed side moves first, offering a menu designed so that each type picks the option meant for it. Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz (1976) showed how this works in insurance: a company offers full coverage at a high price and partial coverage (a big deductible) at a low price. Risky customers take full coverage; safe customers accept the deductible because they rarely claim. They also showed something surprising: in a competitive insurance market there can never be a pooling equilibrium, and sometimes there is no equilibrium at all.

    Airlines, software firms and carmakers do the same thing with quality. Below, you sell two kinds of tickets to two kinds of travelers. A low-value traveler values each unit of comfort at $100, a high-value one at $200. Comfort q costs you 100 × q²/2 dollars to provide. You can’t tell travelers apart, so each buys whichever ticket gives her the most value for the price.

    The best menu has two famous features. No distortion at the top: business class gets exactly the comfort that’s efficient for high-value travelers (q = 2, where the value of more comfort equals its cost). Distortion at the bottom: economy gets less comfort than would be efficient, on purpose, to make it unattractive to high-value travelers, which lets you charge them more for business. High-value travelers still keep some surplus, their information rent: the price of their private information.

    qH = θH,   qL = θL − μ/(1 − μ) × (θH − θL) (or 0 if that is negative),   tL = θLqL,   tH = θHqH − (θH − θL)qL
    Worked example: 30% business travelers

    With θL = 1, θH = 2 (in $100s per unit of comfort) and μ = 0.3: qL = 1 − (0.3/0.7) × 1 ≈ 0.571, sold at tL ≈ $57; business has qH = 2, sold at tH = 4 − 0.571 ≈ 3.43, or $343. Profit per traveler: 0.7 × (57.1 − 16.3) + 0.3 × (342.9 − 200) ≈ $71. If you could tell travelers apart you would earn $95. The $24 gap is the cost of not knowing who is who: partly the information rent of $57 per business traveler, partly the comfort withheld from economy.

    Try this: drag economy (E) to the right, giving it more comfort. At some point the high-value traveler’s dashed line shows that she’d rather buy E, and your profit drops. Then raise the share of business travelers above 50%: the best menu drops economy altogether, because serving low-value travelers costs more in lost business-class revenue than it brings in.

    A little history

    Michael Spence developed job-market signaling in his Harvard doctoral thesis and published it as “Job Market Signaling” in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1973. He shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz.

    Joseph Stiglitz and Michael Rothschild’s “Equilibrium in Competitive Insurance Markets: An Essay on the Economics of Imperfect Information” (QJE, 1976) founded screening theory. Michael Mussa and Sherwin Rosen (1978) and Eric Maskin and John Riley (1984) worked out the monopoly menu used here.

    Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel published “Strategic Information Transmission” in Econometrica in 1982. In-Koo Cho and David Kreps introduced the intuitive criterion in “Signaling Games and Stable Equilibria” (QJE, 1987).

    In biology, Amotz Zahavi argued in 1975 that a peacock’s tail works as a signal of fitness precisely because it is a handicap only a healthy bird can afford. Alan Grafen gave the idea a game-theoretic footing in 1990.

    Where signals and screens show up

    • Warranties and return policies. A long warranty costs a maker of shoddy goods far more than a maker of reliable ones, so offering one is believable. Generous return policies work the same way for retailers.
    • Costly commitments. Patagonia’s 2011 “Don’t Buy This Jacket” ad only made sense for a company prepared to back it with repairs and durable products; see Patagonia Says ‘Don’t Buy This Jacket’. Johnson & Johnson’s $100 million Tylenol recall in 1982 signaled how seriously it took safety; see the Tylenol case.
    • Advertising. Phillip Nelson (1974) and Paul Milgrom and John Roberts (1986) argued that lavish ads for a new product can signal quality: only a firm expecting repeat buyers can afford to spend so much attracting first-time ones.
    • Menus. Airline fare classes, software “basic” and “pro” editions, and membership fees (see Costco’s membership model) let customers sort themselves.
    • Insurance. Deductibles screen safe drivers from risky ones, as Rothschild and Stiglitz predicted.

    Practice

    1. Productivities are $30k and $50k, and a year of school costs a low type $10k and a high type $4k. Which requirements separate the types?

    Show the answer

    Δ = 20, so e* must be at least 20/10 = 2 years (low types won’t copy) and at most 20/4 = 5 years (high types still bother). Any requirement from 2 to 5 years works; the intuitive criterion picks 2.

    2. Same numbers, with a quarter of workers high types. Up to how many years of school can everyone be pooled?

    Show the answer

    The average wage is 0.25 × 50 + 0.75 × 30 = $35k. Low types must prefer it to $30k with no school: 35 − 10e ≥ 30, so e ≤ 0.5 years. That is λΔ/cL = 0.25 × 20/10.

    3. A firm says its product is “the best on the market”. A rival offers a lifetime replacement guarantee. Which is a credible signal of quality, and why?

    Show the answer

    The guarantee. The claim costs nothing for any firm to make, so it can’t separate good from bad. The guarantee is far more expensive for a firm whose products break often, which is exactly the cost difference a signal needs.

    4. With the chapter’s starting numbers (θL = 40, θH = 70, cL = 20, half high types), find the semi-separating equilibrium with a degree of 1.25 years.

    Show the answer

    Low types must be indifferent, so the degree pays 40 + 20 × 1.25 = $65k. Then 70μ + 40(1 − μ) = 65 gives μ = 5/6. Bayes: 0.5/(0.5 + 0.5π) = 5/6, so π = 0.2. A fifth of the low types go to school.

    5. In the cheap-talk model with b = 0.05, find the most informative equilibrium.

    Show the answer

    We need 2 × 0.05 × N(N − 1) < 1, so N(N − 1) < 10: N = 3 works (6), N = 4 doesn’t (12). Boundaries: a1 = 1/3 + 0.1 × 1 × (−2) ≈ 0.133 and a2 = 2/3 + 0.1 × 2 × (−1) ≈ 0.467. Zones: 0–0.133, 0.133–0.467, 0.467–1, each wider than the one before by 4b = 0.2. Your average squared miss is about 0.016.

    6. In the ticket problem, use 20% high-value travelers. Find the best menu and the profit per traveler.

    Show the answer

    qL = 1 − (0.2/0.8) × 1 = 0.75, tL = 0.75; qH = 2, tH = 4 − 0.75 = 3.25. Profit: 0.8 × (0.75 − 0.28125) + 0.2 × (3.25 − 2) = 0.375 + 0.25 = 0.625, or $62.50 per traveler, against $80 with perfect information.

    7. (Harder) Derive the best two-type menu. Show that only the low type’s participation and the high type’s incentive constraint bind, and find qL.

    Show the answer

    Maximize (1 − μ)(tL − qL²/2) + μ(tH − qH²/2) subject to participation (θq − t ≥ 0 for each type) and incentive constraints. The high type can always buy the low ticket and get (θH − θL)qL + (θLqL − tL) ≥ 0, so her participation is automatic once the low type’s holds and her incentive constraint holds. Raise prices until tL = θLqL and tH = θHqH − (θH − θL)qL. Substitute: profit = (1 − μ)(θLqL − qL²/2) + μ(θHqH − qH²/2 − (θH − θL)qL). The first-order conditions give qH = θH and (1 − μ)(θL − qL) = μ(θH − θL), so qL = θL − μ(θH − θL)/(1 − μ). One checks that the low type’s incentive constraint then holds because qH ≥ qL.

    How the interactive works

    The job market. Employers use a step belief: anyone with at least the required education is paid the degree wage; anyone below is treated as a low type. With that belief, the only sensible choices are no school or exactly the requirement. Each type picks the better one (in semi-separating mode, low types are made indifferent and the share π who go to school is found by Bayes’ rule). The rooms then check whether the wage paid matches the average productivity of the workers in them.

    Cheap talk uses the standard uniform-quadratic case: the state is uniform from 0 to 1, losses are squared distances, and the advisor’s favorite is θ + b. The menu uses a buyer value θq − t and a cost q²/2; each traveler buys the ticket with the highest value, or nothing.

    What’s simplified. Two types instead of a continuum, education that is useless for productivity, and employers who compete away all profit. The intuitive criterion is applied only to the simple step beliefs shown.

    How it was checked (Node.js tests, run before the page was built):

    • Scanning requirements in steps of 0.01 years: separating equilibria exist exactly from 1.50 to 3.00 years, pooling ones up to 0.75; with equal costs, only the knife edge of 1.5 years (both types indifferent) remains.
    • Semi-separating at 0.8, 1, 1.25 and 1.4 years: π = 0.875, 0.5, 0.2 and 0.071; in each, the wage equals the Bayes posterior expected productivity and low types are exactly indifferent.
    • Intuitive criterion, scanning all three modes in steps of 0.05: it passes only at 1.5 years, the Riley outcome.
    • No education level from 0 to 5 years (steps of 0.01) gives either type a profitable deviation at a requirement of 2 years.
    • Cheap talk: the most zones are 1, 2, 3 and 7 for b = 0.3, 0.2, 0.05 and 0.01; at every boundary the advisor is indifferent between the two neighboring choices (error below 10−16); 400,000 random draws gave an average loss of 0.01597 against the formula’s 0.01593.
    • Menus: for μ = 0.2, 0.3, 0.5 and 0.6, a grid search and 200,000 random two-ticket menus never beat the formula’s profit (0.625, 0.714, 1.000 and 1.200).

    Check yourself

    1. What makes a costly action a credible signal?

      Show the answer

      Single crossing: there has to be a level that the genuine type will pay for and the pretender won’t. If the cost is the same for both, anything one can afford, so can the other.

    2. Productivities are $40k and $70k, school costs $20k a year for low types and $10k for high types. Which requirement is a separating equilibrium?

      Show the answer

      Separation needs 30/20 = 1.5 to 30/10 = 3 years. At 1 year, low types would copy; at 4, high types would skip school.

    3. What’s the difference between signaling and screening?

      Show the answer

      A job seeker choosing a degree is signaling. An insurer offering a menu of deductibles is screening.

    4. An advisor’s bias grows. What happens to how much she can credibly tell you?

      Show the answer

      The number of credible zones falls as the bias grows, and with b ≥ 1/4 only babbling remains.

    5. In the best ticket menu, which traveler gets less comfort than would be efficient?

      Show the answer

      No distortion at the top, distortion at the bottom: economy is made worse on purpose so that business travelers don’t trade down.
