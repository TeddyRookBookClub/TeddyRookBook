What the Five Foot Shelf is

Charles W. Eliot was president of Harvard for forty years, from 1869 to 1909. He liked to say that a shelf of books about five feet long could hold a liberal education. When he retired, the publisher P. F. Collier & Son took him at his word. With William Allan Neilson, a Harvard professor of English, Eliot chose the works, and Collier printed them in 1909 and 1910 as The Harvard Classics: fifty volumes, from Plato, the Bible and the Greek plays to Shakespeare, Franklin, Darwin and the founding documents of the United States. A volume of lectures on the set followed in 1914.

Eliot’s plan

Eliot believed that anyone who read these books with care, even for only fifteen minutes a day, could gain the essentials of a liberal education. The guide, Fifteen Minutes a Day: The Reading Guide, turns that promise into a calendar. Each day has a few pages, often tied to the date by a birthday or an anniversary, and a short introduction meant to make you want to read on. In a year you sample nearly the whole shelf: history, poetry, philosophy, science, travel, scripture and drama, a little at a time.

How to use the daily readings

  • Today’s reading follows the date on your own phone or computer, wherever you are.
  • The calendar has every day of the year, so you can catch up, read ahead or start on any day you like.
  • Each day opens with the guide’s introduction, then the reading itself, set like a book page: verse stays verse, and the footnotes come at the end.
  • When the guide’s pages begin or end in the middle of a paragraph, the reading runs on to the end of it, and a small note says so.
  • Each day names its volume and pages and links to the scanned page, so you can follow along in your own set or check a word.
  • The day pages print cleanly, one reading to a few sheets of paper.
  • Your browser remembers the last reading you finished (on this device only), so this page can show you where to pick up.
