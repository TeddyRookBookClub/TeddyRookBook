The Federalist Papers

Eighty-five newspaper essays, written in 1787 and 1788 to persuade New York to ratify the Constitution. They are still the most famous explanation of how the Constitution was meant to work. Here is a guide to who wrote them and why, what they argue, and the full text of every paper, with an introduction and the Anti-Federalist essays that argued the other side.

What they were

On September 17, 1787, the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia finished its work and sent the proposed Constitution to the states. It would take effect when conventions in nine of the thirteen states ratified it. In New York the fight was hard. Governor George Clinton and many of the state’s leaders opposed the plan, and newspaper essays signed “Cato” and “Brutus” were already arguing against it.

Alexander Hamilton answered with a series of essays signed “Publius,” after Publius Valerius Publicola, an early Roman who was remembered as a founder of the Roman republic. He recruited James Madison and John Jay to help. The first essay appeared in the Independent Journal on October 27, 1787. Over the next seven months the essays ran in three New York newspapers, the Independent Journal, the New-York Packet and the Daily Advertiser, often several a week, and some were reprinted in other states.

In 1788 the printers J. and A. McLean collected them as a book, The Federalist, in two volumes. The first came out in March 1788 with Nos. 1 to 36. The second, on May 28, 1788, held the rest, including eight new essays (Nos. 78 to 85) that had not yet appeared in the papers. The book’s order and numbering are the ones used today.

The essays were written fast, for a political campaign, by men who were also busy with other work. Even so, readers then and since have found in them a careful account of the reasons behind the Constitution’s design. They are not neutral: they were written to win a vote. Reading the Anti-Federalists alongside them shows what the arguments were about.

New York’s convention met in Poughkeepsie in June 1788, with Anti-Federalists in the majority. By the time it voted, ten other states had ratified, and on July 26, 1788, New York ratified 30 to 27. Historians still discuss how much the essays changed anyone’s vote. Their larger influence came later, as a guide to the Constitution’s meaning.

The authors

Alexander Hamilton (1755 or 1757–1804)

A New York lawyer and former aide to General Washington, and one of New York’s three delegates at the Convention. He planned the series and wrote 51 of the essays, including most of those on defense, taxes, the presidency and the courts. He became the first Secretary of the Treasury.

James Madison (1751–1836)

A Virginian who had studied the history of republics and confederacies before the Convention and kept the fullest notes of its debates. He was in New York as a member of the Confederation Congress. He wrote 29 essays by the usual count, including Nos. 10 and 51. He later proposed the Bill of Rights in the First Congress and became the fourth President.

John Jay (1745–1829)

A New York lawyer and diplomat who had helped negotiate the 1783 peace treaty with Britain and was the Confederation’s Secretary for Foreign Affairs. He wrote Nos. 2 to 5 on foreign dangers, then fell ill, and later wrote No. 64 on treaties. He became the first Chief Justice of the United States.

Nos. 18 to 20 were written jointly, mostly from Madison’s notes on earlier confederacies, with material from Hamilton; they are usually counted with Madison’s. The essays appeared anonymously. Many readers guessed who the authors were, but lists of who wrote which papers became public only after Hamilton’s death in 1804.

The disputed papers, and how scholars settled them

For a century and a half, no one could say for sure who wrote twelve of the essays: Nos. 49 to 58, 62 and 63. The trouble came from two lists. Shortly before his fatal duel with Aaron Burr in 1804, Hamilton left a memorandum claiming those twelve as his own. (His list also gave No. 54 to Jay by mistake; Jay wrote No. 64.) Years later Madison claimed the same twelve. His list was used in the 1818 edition printed by Jacob Gideon, and he said Hamilton’s list must have been made out in a hurry. Both men were careful writers, and both had reasons to remember accurately, so for a long time historians took sides.

In 1944 the historian Douglass Adair argued from the history of the essays and their ideas that Madison wrote all twelve. Some of the arguments in them match Madison’s known views and his notes from the Convention closely.

Then in 1963 and 1964 two statisticians, Frederick Mosteller and David L. Wallace, tried a new kind of test. Writers use small, common words at steady, personal rates without thinking about it. Hamilton, for example, wrote “upon” far more often than Madison did. Mosteller and Wallace measured how often each man used dozens of such words in essays whose authorship was certain, then used probability (a Bayesian analysis) to compare the twelve disputed essays. Their result: every one of the twelve was much more likely to be Madison’s. The evidence was weakest for No. 55, but it still pointed to Madison. Their study, published as Inference and Disputed Authorship: The Federalist (1964), became a famous early example of using statistics to study writing.

What is agreed: most historians and nearly all later statistical studies, using different methods, credit Madison with the twelve papers, which gives the usual count of 51 for Hamilton, 29 for Madison and 5 for Jay. What is not agreed: some scholars argue that the two men worked together on more papers than the lists show, so that some essays may be partly collaborations; and the exact share of each in the joint papers 18 to 20 is still discussed. On this site, the twelve are marked “disputed” and credited to Madison, as most scholars do.

The plan of the work

In No. 1 Hamilton promised six topics: the usefulness of the Union; the failures of the Articles of Confederation; the need for an energetic government; the plan’s agreement with republican principles; its likeness to New York’s own constitution; and the security it would give to liberty and property. The series grew as it went. The fourth topic became a long tour of the Constitution, part by part, and the last two were touched on only in No. 85. Here is how the papers fall into groups.

Key ideas

Faction
A group, majority or minority, moved by a shared passion or interest against the rights of others or the good of the whole. Madison thought its causes, above all differences in property, are permanent, so a constitution must control its effects. No. 10
The extended republic
Against the common view that republics must be small, Madison argued that a large one is safer: elected representatives refine public views, and the many interests in a large country make it hard for one faction to form a majority. Anti-Federalists replied that a large republic would be distant and would need force. No. 10, 14, 51
Federalism
Power divided between the national government, with listed powers, and the states, which keep the rest. Madison called the plan partly national and partly federal. No. 39, 45, 46
Separation of powers
Legislative, executive and judicial power held by different hands, because putting them together is “the very definition of tyranny.” Some overlap is allowed and even needed. No. 47, 48
Checks and balances
Each branch gets the means and the motive to resist the others: “ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” Examples: the veto, Senate consent to appointments, impeachment, two houses of Congress. No. 51
Energy in the executive
A single President, with a fixed term, a secure salary and real powers, so the government can act with “decision, activity, secrecy, and despatch,” and so the public knows whom to hold responsible. No. 70
Judicial review
Courts must treat the Constitution as a higher law and refuse to apply acts that contradict it; this places the people’s will, expressed in the Constitution, above both legislature and courts. The Supreme Court adopted similar reasoning in Marbury v. Madison (1803). No. 78
The case against a bill of rights
Hamilton argued that a government of listed powers needs no list of rights, and that such a list might imply powers never granted. The Anti-Federalists won this argument in practice: the Bill of Rights was ratified in 1791, and the Ninth Amendment answers Hamilton’s worry. No. 84

How courts and scholars use them today

Judges, lawyers and historians read The Federalist as evidence of what the Constitution meant to people in 1787 and 1788. Supreme Court Justices have quoted it from the Court’s early years to the present; one scholarly count found it cited in 291 decisions by the year 2000, with Nos. 42, 78, 81, 51 and 32 among the most cited. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall wrote that its authors’ opinions deserved “great respect,” while the Court kept the right to judge whether they were correct.

Justices of very different views cite it, sometimes in the same case. In Printz v. United States (1997), for example, the majority and the dissent both relied on passages from The Federalist (including No. 27) to reach opposite conclusions about whether Congress could require state officers to carry out a federal program.

How much weight the essays deserve is itself debated. Judges and scholars who look to the Constitution’s original meaning often treat them as strong evidence of how informed people understood the text. Others caution that they were persuasive pieces written quickly for one state’s debate, that they speak for three men rather than all the framers or ratifiers, that the authors sometimes disagreed with each other later, and that many ratifying conventions never read them. Both views are held by serious scholars, and readers can weigh the essays as they would any primary source: by asking who wrote them, when, for whom, and why.

A reading plan

The whole work is about 190,000 words, roughly 14 hours of reading. A good plan is one or two papers a day, with an Anti-Federalist piece each week. Use the paragraph numbers to mark passages and the questions at the top of each paper to check your understanding.

Study questions

  1. Why did Hamilton choose to write as “Publius” instead of under his own name? What are the advantages and risks of anonymous political writing?
  2. Explain Madison’s argument in No. 10 that a large republic controls faction better than a small one. Then explain Brutus’s argument that a free republic cannot work over a large territory. Which do you find more convincing, and why?
  3. What is the difference between separation of powers (No. 47) and checks and balances (No. 51)? Give two examples of each from the Constitution.
  4. Hamilton says the courts are “the least dangerous” branch (No. 78). Brutus said they would be the most dangerous. Make the best case for each view.
  5. Read No. 84 and Federal Farmer IV. Why did the Federalists at first oppose a bill of rights, and why did the Anti-Federalists insist on one? How did the Ninth Amendment respond to Hamilton’s worry?
  6. How did statisticians help settle who wrote the disputed papers? What can and can’t word counts prove?
  7. The Federalist was written to win a political argument. How should that affect the way judges and historians use it? Present two different views.
  8. Which of the Federalists’ predictions came true, and which did not? Consider the House, the Electoral College, the states’ power, and the courts.
