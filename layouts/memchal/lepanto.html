Chesterton’s Lepanto tells the story of a sea battle of 1571 in drums, trumpets and galloping rhythm, and it has long been a poem people learn by heart and say out loud. It is long, about ten minutes from first line to last, but it is built for memory: eight vivid scenes, a refrain that keeps coming back (“Don John of Austria …”), strong rhymes in pairs, and lines that sound like the things they describe. Learn it a few lines at a time, and in a few months the whole thing is yours.

How to learn it

The poem comes in {{ len $D.chunks }} chunks of four to seven lines, one small bite of the {{ len $D.scenes }} scenes. For each chunk: read it aloud and picture it; say it from first letters only; fill in the gaps as more and more words disappear; then recall it with nothing showing, by typing or saying it. Once you get a chunk word for word it is learned, and it comes back for review on day 1, 3, 7 and 21. Every so often, chain it to the chunks before it, so each line pulls the next. Last of all, the recital: the whole poem, aloud, against the clock.

A memory palace for Lepanto

The poem’s eight scenes jump from place to place: the Sultan’s court, a winding hill road, a fairy-tale paradise, a mountain in France, a king’s closet, the Pope’s chapel, the battle itself, and Cervantes on his galley. A memory palace keeps them in order. Pick a walk you know with your eyes shut, such as the rooms of your home, and put one scene in each room, as big and strange as you can make it. Then, before each scene, walk into its room in your mind, and the first lines will be waiting. Here is one way to set it up in a house; your own pictures will stick better. The refrain helps too. Five scenes end with a short four-line refrain, closing on “Don John of Austria” and what he is doing now: riding to the sea (II), gone by Alcalar (IV), shouting to the ships (V), loosing the cannonade (VI) and setting his people free (VII). The last line of all brings him home from the Crusade. Say those endings in order and you have the poem’s skeleton.

About the poem

The poet

G. K. Chesterton (1874–1936) was an English writer of almost everything: newspaper columns, essays, novels, the Father Brown detective stories, books on faith such as Orthodoxy (1908), and poems. He wrote Lepanto in 1911, when he was 37; it first appeared that October in The Eye-Witness, a London weekly, and was collected in his Poems of 1915. He joined the Catholic Church in 1922, eleven years after writing it.

The battle

On 7 October 1571 AD, two great fleets of oared warships, called galleys, met in the Gulf of Patras off western Greece. On one side was the Holy League, an alliance of Spain, the Republic of Venice, the Papal States and other Catholic states, formed by Pope Pius V after the Ottoman Empire attacked Venetian Cyprus in 1570. On the other was the Ottoman fleet under Ali Pasha. The League’s commander was Don John of Austria, half-brother of King Philip II of Spain, then 24 years old. More than 200 galleys fought on each side. The League won: most of the Ottoman galleys were captured or sunk, Ali Pasha was killed, and thousands of Christian captives who had been rowing the Ottoman ships were set free. Losses were heavy on both sides. The Ottomans rebuilt their fleet within about a year, and in 1573 Venice made peace and gave up Cyprus. A young Spanish soldier named Miguel de Cervantes fought on the League side and was wounded; he later wrote Don Quixote, and the poem ends with him.

Whose story it tells

The poem tells the battle from the Holy League’s side, as its author saw it in 1911, and its pictures of the Ottomans and of Islam come from old European legend and insult (such as the medieval name “Mahound”), not from what Muslims believe (Grokipedia: Mahound).

Who has known it by heart?

  • John Buchan, the Scottish novelist, wrote to Chesterton in June 1915, during the First World War: “The other day in the trenches we shouted your Lepanto.” (Quoted in Ian Ker’s biography of Chesterton, 2011.)
  • J. R. R. Tolkien admired the poem, and his daughter Priscilla remembered that he enjoyed reciting it (Holly Ordway, “Tolkien’s Reading of Chesterton”). We found no record that says whether he could say all {{ $n }} lines from memory.
  • Alice Roosevelt Longworth, Theodore Roosevelt’s daughter, is said to have known the whole poem by heart. It is a good story, but we could not find it in a source we could check, so we leave it as a story.
  • How long it takes: the poem has {{ lang.FormatNumber 0 $D.words }} words. Dramatic poetry is usually read aloud at about 110 to 150 words a minute, which gives 9 to 12 minutes; a brisk, galloping recital takes about 8. The LibriVox recording of Chesterton’s Poems gives Lepanto a track of 9 minutes 40 seconds, short spoken introduction included.
