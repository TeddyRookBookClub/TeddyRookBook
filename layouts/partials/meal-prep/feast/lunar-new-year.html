🥟 How to fold dumplings

Set up a station: wrappers under a damp towel, the filling, a small bowl of water and a tray dusted with flour. Use a scant tablespoon of filling; too much and the dumpling won’t seal. Three folds, from easiest to fanciest:

1. Half moon (start here)

  1. Filling in the center.
  2. Wet half the rim with a fingertip of water.
  3. Fold in half and press the edges together from one end to the other, pushing out air.

2. Pleated crescent

  1. Fold in half and pinch only the top center.
  2. On one side, make 3 or 4 small pleats in the front layer, pressing each against the flat back layer.
  3. Repeat on the other side. The dumpling curves and sits up on its flat bottom, perfect for potstickers.

3. Ingot

  1. Make a sealed half moon.
  2. Bend the two corners down and toward each other under the filling.
  3. Overlap them with a dab of water and pinch. It looks like an old gold or silver ingot, which is why dumplings stand for wealth.

🧧 What the foods mean

Many New Year foods carry wishes, often because their names sound like lucky words in Chinese or because of their shape. Families keep different customs, and what’s served in the north differs from the south. These are the meanings most widely recorded:

FoodWhat it stands forWhy
🥟 Dumplings (jiaozi)WealthTheir shape looks like old gold and silver ingots. Families fold them together on New Year’s Eve, especially in northern China, and some hide a coin in one for luck.
🍜 Long noodlesLong lifeThe long strands are served uncut; cutting them would “cut” the wish short.
🐟 Whole fishPlentyThe word for fish (yú) sounds like the word for surplus. It’s served whole, and some families leave a little for “surplus every year.”
🍘 Nian gaoA better yearThe name sounds like “higher year,” a wish for growth and success; its stickiness stands for a close family.
⚪ TangyuanReunionThe name sounds like the word for reunion, and the round shape means togetherness. They’re eaten on the Lantern Festival, the fifteenth day.
🌯 Spring rollsWealthFried golden, they look like bars of gold.
🐔 Whole chickenFamily togethernessServed whole, the bird stands for a complete family.

📜 The holiday in brief

Lunar New Year begins with the new moon that opens the first month of the traditional lunisolar calendar, so it falls between January 21 and February 20. In China it’s called the Spring Festival. Celebrations last fifteen days, from the reunion dinner on New Year’s Eve, when families travel home to eat together, to the Lantern Festival. Each year is named for one of twelve animals in turn.

Other countries and communities keep their own New Year traditions on or near the same day: in Korea, Seollal, with a soup of sliced rice cakes (tteokguk) that marks everyone growing a year older; in Vietnam, Tết, with square sticky rice cakes (bánh chưng). This collection follows Chinese customs, and even those vary widely from family to family.

🌡 Food safety

  • Pork dumplings and meatballs: ground pork is done at 160°F. Cut a dumpling open: no pink, steaming hot (USDA).
  • Whole chicken: 165°F in the thickest part of the thigh and the breast (USDA). Poach gently and check before chilling.
  • Fish: 145°F, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork (FDA).
  • Raw filling: keep it cold while you fold, wash hands and boards after touching raw pork, and refrigerate trays of folded dumplings if the room is warm.
  • Frying spring rolls: stay with the hot oil the whole time. If a pan catches fire, slide a lid over it and turn off the burner; never use water (Los Angeles Fire Department).
  • Sticky rice treats like nian gao and tangyuan are very chewy. Cut them small for young children and eat slowly.
  • Leftovers: refrigerate within 2 hours and eat within 3 to 4 days; reheat to 165°F.
