🎨 Dyeing eggs with food-safe colors

Start with hard-cooked eggs that have no cracks, cooled and dry. Use food coloring, egg-dye kits sold for eggs, or dyes from food, never paints or craft dyes (USDA advises food-grade dye only).

With food coloring

  1. For each color, stir ½ cup boiling water, 1 teaspoon white vinegar and 10 to 20 drops of food coloring in a cup. A grown-up pours the boiling water.
  2. If you like, draw on the egg first with a white crayon; the wax keeps the dye off and the drawing shows through.
  3. Lower an egg in on a spoon and leave it 3 to 5 minutes; longer makes a deeper color. Dip half in one color and half in another for two-tone eggs.
  4. Lift it out and dry it on a rack or an egg carton.
  5. Put the eggs back in the refrigerator as soon as they’re dry. All the time out of the refrigerator counts toward 2 hours.

With dyes from the kitchen

Simmer the food in 4 cups water with 2 tablespoons vinegar for 30 minutes, strain and cool. These colors are softer and need a longer soak, so soak the eggs in the refrigerator (a few hours or overnight).

Orange to rust skins from 6 yellow onions
Yellow 3 tablespoons ground turmeric
Pink 4 cups chopped beets
Tan to brown 4 cups strong coffee (no water)

🌡 Food safety: eggs, ham and lamb

Eggs (USDA and FDA)

  • Refrigerate hard-cooked eggs within 2 hours of cooking and eat them within 1 week.
  • Never leave cooked eggs out of the refrigerator more than 2 hours in all (1 hour above 90°F). That includes dyeing, display and the hunt.
  • Don’t dye or eat eggs with cracked shells.
  • Hide eggs away from dirt, pets and lawn chemicals. Throw out any found hours later or the next day.
  • FDA advises not eating eggs after hiding or playing with them. The simplest plan: hunt with plastic eggs, or with a separate batch, and eat the ones that stayed cold.
  • Wash hands before and after handling eggs.

Which ham do you have?

  • Fully cooked (most hams, including spiral-cut): safe cold. To serve hot, heat to 140°F at 325°F, 10 to 18 minutes per pound for spiral ham, 18 to 24 for a bone-in half ham.
  • Cook before eating: cook to 145°F and rest at least 3 minutes, 22 to 25 minutes per pound for a half ham.
  • Leftovers from a ham you heated keep 3 to 5 days in the refrigerator, or 1 to 2 months frozen. Reheat to 165°F.

Lamb

A leg of lamb is done at 145°F with a 3-minute rest (medium-rare), 160°F for medium. Allow 20 to 26 minutes per pound at 325°F (USDA).

📜 Where the traditions come from

Easter is the Christian feast of the Resurrection of Jesus. In Western churches it falls on the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after March 21, so it moves between March 22 and April 25; many Eastern Orthodox churches use the older Julian calendar and often celebrate later. Many families also keep spring customs that aren’t religious, like egg hunts.

  • Eggs have long stood for new life in spring. Decorating them is an old custom across Europe and the Middle East, from simple dyed eggs to the painted eggs of Ukraine and Poland.
  • Hot cross buns are tied to Good Friday in Britain. In 1592, under Queen Elizabeth I, a London rule allowed spiced buns to be sold only on Good Friday, at Christmas and for burials.
  • Lamb is the traditional Easter meat in much of Europe and the Middle East. Ham became the common American choice, partly because hams cured over the winter were ready in spring.
