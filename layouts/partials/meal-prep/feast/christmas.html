📦 Packing, storing and giving cookies

Storing

  • Cool completely before packing; warm cookies steam and go soft.
  • Keep crisp and soft cookies apart. Soft ones give up moisture and turn crisp ones soft.
  • Airtight tins or containers, with parchment between layers of iced or sticky cookies.
  • Most baked cookies keep about a week at room temperature; shortbread keeps two. Anything with cream cheese frosting or a cream filling goes in the refrigerator.
  • Freeze baked cookies or dough up to 3 months for best quality. Thaw cookies in their closed container so they don’t sweat.

Packing and giving

  • Pack the heaviest cookies at the bottom and the most delicate on top.
  • For mailing, choose sturdy bars and drop cookies, wrap them in pairs back to back, and pad the box so nothing shifts.
  • Peppermint bark and fudge make good gifts in small tins; marshmallows pair well with a jar of cocoa.
  • Label each gift with what it is and its allergens. The major food allergens in the United States are milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame (FDA).

Baking

  • Don’t taste raw dough or batter. Raw flour and raw eggs can carry germs; bake first (FDA and CDC). That goes for kids’ fingers, too.
  • Royal icing isn’t cooked, so make it with pasteurized egg whites or meringue powder (FDA advises pasteurized eggs for raw-egg recipes).
  • Custards and egg fillings, like the trifle’s, cook to 160°F.

After dinner

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours, in shallow containers. Use them within 3 to 4 days, or freeze them; leftover ham keeps 3 to 5 days. Reheat to 165°F and bring gravy to a rolling boil (USDA).

📜 Where the traditions come from

Christmas, on December 25, is the Christian celebration of the birth of Jesus, and it is also a cultural holiday for many families who mark it in their own ways. Its baking traditions grew up in different countries over centuries:

  • Gingerbread houses were made in Germany before the Brothers Grimm published Hansel and Gretel in 1812, but the story’s candy cottage made them famous. German immigrants brought the custom to America in the 1800s.
  • Royal icing takes its name from its use on Queen Victoria’s wedding cake in 1840.
  • Spritz cookies come from the German Spritzgebäck, from spritzen, “to squirt,” for the cookie press.
  • Yorkshire pudding first appears in print in 1737 as “a dripping pudding,” baked under roasting meat to catch its fat. Hannah Glasse gave it its present name in 1747.
  • The bûche de Noël recalls the Yule log once burned on the hearth. Paris pastry cooks turned it into a cake in the late 1800s.
