🌡 Party food safety, from kickoff to the final whistle

A game lasts longer than the 2-hour rule

Pregame, four quarters and halftime add up to four hours or more, but perishable food shouldn’t sit out longer than 2 hours. So plan in waves:

  • Put out food in batches. Keep the rest hot in the oven or cold in the refrigerator, and bring out fresh platters around halftime.
  • After 2 hours at room temperature, refrigerate dishes or throw them out; don’t just top up an old bowl.
  • Keep hot dips and chili in a slow cooker on warm or low (140°F or above).
  • Set cold dips in a bowl nested in a bigger bowl of ice, and swap the ice as it melts.
  • Snack mix, chips, pretzels and cookies can stay out all game; they fill the gaps between hot batches.

Frying wings safely

  • Stay with the pot the whole time. If you have to leave the kitchen, turn the burner off.
  • Use a deep, heavy pot with plenty of room above the oil and a deep-fry thermometer. If the oil smokes, turn off the heat.
  • Keep a lid or sheet pan beside the stove. If the oil catches fire, turn off the burner and slide the lid over the pot. Never pour water on a grease fire.
  • Turn pot handles toward the back of the stove, and keep kids back from it.
  • Prefer less fuss? The baked wings come out crisp with no pot of oil.

📜 Where the party food came from

Buffalo wings are usually traced to the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New York, where Teressa Bellissimo is said to have fried chicken wings and tossed them in hot sauce and butter one night in 1964, serving them with celery and blue cheese dressing. Another Buffalo restaurateur, John Young, was serving breaded wings around the same time, so the city has more than one claim.

Nachos are credited to Ignacio “Nacho” Anaya, a restaurant host in Piedras Negras, Mexico, just across the border from Eagle Pass, Texas. In 1943 he topped tortilla chips with cheese and pickled jalapeños for a group of late-arriving guests. They spread to American ballparks and stadiums in the 1970s.

Chili con carne grew up in Texas in the 1800s, and every family seems to have its own version. Beans or no beans is still a friendly argument.
