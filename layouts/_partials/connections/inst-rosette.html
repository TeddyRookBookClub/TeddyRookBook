The orbit, seen from above

The gold dots mark each closest approach. With Newton's inverse-square law they all fall on one spot and the orbit is a closed ellipse. With any other law the closest point steps round the center, and the orbit draws a rosette.

The ladder behind the rosette

fractionfigurehow nearly it closesnext term

Between one closest approach and the next, the body goes round the center by a fixed amount. Written as a fraction of a turn, that amount has a continued fraction, and its rungs are the figures the rosette almost draws: a fraction p/q is a flower of q petals drawn in p turns. A large next term means the flower nearly closes before it starts to turn.

What is known and what is ours. The rule for nearly round orbits is Newton's (Principia, Book I, Proposition 45): if the pull weakens as distance to the power p, the closest and farthest points are 180°/√(3 − p) apart. That only two laws close every orbit, the inverse square and the spring, is Bertrand's theorem (1873). Reading the step as a continued fraction, and the rosette's flowers as its rungs, is this project's addition. The orbits here are computed step by step in your browser.
