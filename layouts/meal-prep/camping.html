🔥 Campfire and camp-stove safety

The campfire (Smokey Bear, USDA Forest Service)

  • Check the rules first. Campgrounds and parks post fire restrictions; during a fire ban, cook on a stove.
  • Use an established fire ring or fire pit where one exists. Keep the fire at least 15 feet from tents, bushes and low branches, with open sky above.
  • Have water and a shovel ready before you light it.
  • Keep it small, and burn only wood. Never burn trash, cans, glass, batteries or aerosol cans.
  • Never leave it, even for a minute. A grown-up stays with the fire, and kids stay a safe distance back.
  • Put it out completely: drown it with water until the hissing stops, stir the ashes and embers with the shovel, drown it again, and feel for heat with the back of your hand held above the ashes. If it’s too hot to touch, it’s too hot to leave.

Camp stoves, grills and carbon monoxide (CDC)

Never use a camp stove, charcoal grill, hibachi or fuel-burning lantern inside a tent, camper or other enclosed space. They give off carbon monoxide, an odorless gas that can build up in enclosed or partly enclosed spaces and is deadly. Cook outdoors, in the open air.

Cooking over coals

  • Cook over glowing coals, not tall flames; they give steady heat and burn food less.
  • Use long tongs, a lid lifter for the Dutch oven, and heat-proof gloves.
  • Open foil packets away from your face: the steam is very hot.
  • Set the stove on a flat, stable surface, and let it cool before you pack it.
  • Use a food thermometer: ground beef 160°F, chicken 165°F, steaks and chops 145°F with a 3-minute rest, precooked sausages and hot dogs 165°F (USDA).

🧊 Keeping the cooler cold (USDA)

  • Keep perishable food at 40°F or below. A cooler thermometer tells you.
  • Block ice lasts longer than cubes. Freeze water in clean jugs at home, or use frozen gel packs. Frozen meals (chili, seasoned chicken, patties) work as ice for the first day.
  • Bring two coolers if you can: one for drinks and snacks, which gets opened all day, and one for perishable food, which stays shut.
  • Pack raw meat sealed, at the bottom, below ready-to-eat food, so juices can’t drip on it.
  • Keep the cooler closed, in the shade, and covered with a blanket or tarp. Store food in watertight containers so it doesn’t sit in melt water.
  • When the ice is gone, throw out the perishable food; leftovers are safe only while the cooler still has ice.

The 2-hour rule, outdoors

Perishable food shouldn’t sit out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour when it’s above 90°F. Put it back in the cooler as soon as you’ve served it.

Water

Bring drinking water from home or the campground tap. Don’t drink lake or stream water unless it’s treated: bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute (longer at high elevations), or use a filter rated 1 micron absolute or smaller.

Clean hands

Wash hands before and after handling food. If there’s no running water, bring soap and a jug of water, or disposable wipes.

🌲 Leave No Trace

Leave No Trace’s seven principles are a simple code for enjoying the outdoors without harming it. Here they are, with what they mean in the camp kitchen.

  1. Plan ahead and prepare. Know the site’s rules and fire restrictions. Repackage food at home to cut down on trash.
  2. Travel and camp on durable surfaces. Use existing campsites and trails; camp at least 200 feet from lakes and streams where you can.
  3. Dispose of waste properly. Pack out all trash and leftover food. Wash dishes with a little biodegradable soap, at least 200 feet from water, and scatter the strained dishwater.
  4. Leave what you find. Leave rocks, plants and natural objects as they are.
  5. Minimize campfire impacts. Cook on a stove when you can. Where fires are allowed, use established fire rings, keep fires small, burn only dead and down wood you can break by hand, and put fires out completely.
  6. Respect wildlife. Never feed animals. Store food and trash securely, away from wildlife.
  7. Be considerate of other visitors. Keep noise down and share the space kindly.

Principles from the Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics, as published by the National Park Service.

🍲 How many coals for a Dutch oven?

A camp Dutch oven (the kind with legs and a flat, rimmed lid) bakes with charcoal briquettes underneath and on top. A widely taught rule of thumb: double the oven’s diameter in inches for about 325°F, add or take away 2 briquettes for each 25°F, and for baking put about two-thirds on the lid and one-third underneath (Missouri Department of Conservation). Wind, cold and shade change it, so check early.

…

Light the briquettes in a chimney starter, in the fire ring or on bare ground cleared of anything that burns; they’re ready when coated in gray ash. Turn the oven a quarter turn one way and the lid a quarter turn the other every 15 minutes for even baking. Put spent coals in the fire ring and put them out completely.

📜 About bannock

Bannock began as a flat Scottish bread of oats or barley; the name comes from the Scottish Gaelic bannach, a morsel. Scottish fur traders carried it to North America in the 1700s and 1800s, and many Indigenous communities in Canada and the northern United States made it their own with wheat flour and baking powder, cooking it in skillets or on sticks over the fire. Before that, many Indigenous peoples made their own breads from corn, roots and seeds. Today it remains an everyday bread in many Indigenous communities in Canada and the United States, and a favorite at campfires everywhere.
