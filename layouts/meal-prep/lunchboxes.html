🧊 Keeping packed lunches safe

Cold lunches (USDA)

  • Use an insulated, soft-sided lunch bag or box.
  • Pack at least two cold sources with anything perishable: two frozen gel packs, or one gel pack and a frozen juice box or bottle of water. Put one on top of the food and one underneath.
  • Meat, poultry, eggs, dairy, cut fruit and pasta or grain salads are perishable. Cook and chill them ahead, in the refrigerator, in shallow containers.
  • Whole fruits and vegetables, hard cheese, unopened canned meat or fish, bread, crackers, peanut or seed butter, jelly, mustard and pickles don’t need a cold source.
  • Pack just what your child will eat at lunch. Make it the night before, but put the bag together right before leaving.
  • You can freeze plain sandwiches to help keep them cold; add mayonnaise, lettuce and tomato separately.
  • If there’s a refrigerator at school, store the bag there with the lid open.

Hot lunches (USDA)

  1. Fill the insulated food jar with boiling water, close it and let it stand a few minutes.
  2. Heat the food until it’s steaming, 165°F on a food thermometer.
  3. Empty the jar, fill it with the hot food and keep it closed until lunch, so the food stays at 140°F or above.

After lunch

Throw away leftover food, used bags and wrappers. Don’t reuse packaging (USDA). Wash reusable containers, gel packs and the bag every day.

For little ones

For toddlers and preschoolers, cut grapes, cherry tomatoes and hot dogs into small pieces; whole, they are choking hazards for young children (CDC).

Hands and counters

Wash your hands for 20 seconds before packing, and wash cutting boards and counters between foods (USDA).

🥜 Nut-free schools and allergies

Many schools and classrooms are nut-free because of children with peanut or tree-nut allergies. Follow your school’s policy; it may also cover other foods.

  • Turn on Nut-free school in the rotation to leave out every dish with peanuts or tree nuts. Dishes with nuts are marked “(has nuts)” in the list above.
  • Sunflower seed butter stands in for peanut butter in the roll-ups, energy bites and PB&J. Some schools ask for no look-alike spreads; check first.
  • Read labels every time. Ingredients change. Packaged foods must list the nine major allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame (FDA). Watch for chocolate chips, granola, crackers and cereal bars, which sometimes contain nuts.
  • Each recipe page lists its allergens and swaps for milk, eggs, wheat and more.

📋 Prep ahead: an hour on Sunday

On the weekend

  • Bake one thing: muffins, pizza pinwheels or corn dog muffins. Freeze most of them; a frozen muffin thaws in the lunch box by lunchtime.
  • Cook one big thing: a pasta salad, a grain bowl, or meatballs. Chill it in shallow containers; it keeps 3 to 4 days.
  • Hard-cook six eggs. They keep a week in the refrigerator.
  • Wash and cut carrots, celery, cucumbers and peppers. Store them in containers with a damp paper towel.
  • Mix a batch of energy bites and freeze a pan of yogurt bark.

Each school night (10 minutes)

  • Freeze the gel packs (or a juice box or water bottle) overnight.
  • Make the sandwich or wrap and refrigerate it.
  • Put the extras and fresh food in containers.
  • In the morning: bag it up with the cold sources, or heat the thermos lunch.

Get kids packing

Kids pack lunches they will eat. A simple rule helps: one main, one fruit, one vegetable, one extra. Younger kids can choose from two options you set out; older kids can pack it all.

📜 Lunch boxes, then and now

In the 1800s, miners and factory workers carried their midday meal in tin pails with lids and handles, or in reused lard buckets. In 1950 a metal lunch box decorated with a cowboy hero of radio, film and television became a hit, and for the next two decades picture lunch boxes were a back-to-school tradition in the United States. Plastic boxes and insulated soft bags came later.

In Japan, the bento, a boxed meal of rice and small side dishes, goes back centuries; travelers in the Kamakura period (1185–1333) carried dried rice called hoshi-ii. Today’s divided lunch boxes borrow the idea: small portions of several foods, each in its own section.
