Element sounds

These are artistic mappings: atoms don’t make sounds. Each mode turns real numbers about the element into tones you can hear.

Spectrum chord. Every visible line sounds at once. A line’s light wave vibrates hundreds of trillions of times a second; dividing by 240 moves it exactly 40 octaves down, into the range of a voice, and keeps the musical spacing between the lines. Brighter lines are louder. This is the idea W. Walker Smith presented to the American Chemical Society in 2023, and Jill Linz’s Atom Music at Skidmore College works the same way.

Spectrum arpeggio. The same tones one at a time, from red (lowest) to violet (highest), each plucked and dying away like a string, as in Linz’s atom tones and the melodies Smith drew from the lines.

Electron melody. One piano note for each subshell, in the order electrons fill them. The shell number picks the note of the scale: shell 1 is do, shell 2 is re, up to shell 7, ti. A subshell with more electrons is a longer note. The idea comes from Sam Zhang’s 2006 article “Teaching Electron Configuration the Musical Way.”

The first musical table. In 1865 John Newlands noticed that, listed by weight, every eighth element seemed to repeat the first, like the eighth note of a musical octave. His “law of octaves” was laughed at, but it was an early glimpse of the periodic law. Sweep a group to hear elements of one family one after another.

What you’re seeing

The shell model is the picture most of us first learn: a nucleus of protons (red) and neutrons (gray) in the middle, and electrons (blue) on rings around it. The number on each ring is how many electrons that shell holds. It is good for counting, and it shows why the table has rows: a new row starts each time a new shell starts to fill. But it is not to scale. If the atom were as wide as a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea in the middle.

Electrons don’t orbit like planets. An electron has no path at all. Quantum mechanics only tells us how likely we are to find it in each spot, and that gives a fuzzy cloud called an orbital. In the Orbitals picture, each dot is a place the electron could be found: where the dots are thick, it is often there. Two colors mark the two signs of the electron’s wave, which matter when atoms bond.

The shapes have names. An s orbital is a ball. Each p orbital is a pair of lobes along one axis (px, py, pz). The five d orbitals are mostly four-leaf clovers, and the seven f orbitals are more lobed still. Choose Side by side to see one subshell’s orbitals spread out, and tap any cloud to read its name and how many electrons it holds.

A full subshell is round. Put all three p orbitals together, or all five d orbitals, and the lobes fill each other in to make a sphere. That is why All together looks like nested fuzzy balls for most atoms: atoms really are close to round.

The science

Filling order

Electrons fill subshells from the lowest energy up. A handy rule, the Madelung rule (or Aufbau order), sorts the subshells by n + l, and by n when there’s a tie:

1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p

Here n is the shell number and l is 0, 1, 2, 3 for s, p, d, f. About twenty elements break the rule a little. Chromium is [Ar] 3d5 4s1, not 3d4 4s2, and copper is [Ar] 3d10 4s1. The page uses each element’s measured configuration and marks the exceptions.

Where the shapes come from

For hydrogen, the Schrödinger equation can be solved exactly. Each orbital is a radial part times an angular part:

ψnlm(r, θ, φ) = Rnl(r) · Ylm(θ, φ)

The angular part, a real spherical harmonic, gives the shape: the s, p, d and f lobes. The radial part sets the size and adds n − l − 1 round nodes, shells where the electron is never found. The chance of finding the electron near a point is |ψ|², and the dots are drawn at random with exactly that chance.

Sizes, roughly

In a bigger atom each electron is pulled by the nucleus but pushed by the other electrons, so it feels a smaller effective nuclear charge. In 1930 John Slater gave simple rules to estimate it. For iron’s 4s electrons:

Zeff = 26 − (1 × 0.35 + 14 × 0.85 + 10 × 1.00) = 3.75

Each orbital here uses a hydrogen-like shape, sized so its average radius matches Slater’s estimate. That is an approximation: real many-electron orbitals are close to these shapes, but their sizes differ somewhat, and heavy atoms also feel effects from relativity.

Spectral fingerprints

When an atom’s electron drops from a higher energy level to a lower one, the atom gives off light of one exact color. Each element has its own set of levels, so each has its own pattern of bright lines, as personal as a fingerprint. Astronomers read these lines in starlight to learn what stars are made of; helium was found in the Sun’s spectrum in 1868, before anyone found it on Earth.

Ephoton = Eupper − Elower = hc / λ

Try this

Watch a row grow. Pick sodium and press Next until argon. Each step adds a proton and an electron, and the third shell fills from 1 to 8. Then go on to potassium, where a fourth ring appears.
Find the hidden shapes. Pick carbon, choose Orbitals and Side by side, and look at the 2p subshell: two dumbbells at right angles. Now try neon, where all three are there, and then All together: they make a ball.
Fingerprints side by side. Compare sodium with neon, or iron with copper. Sodium’s brightest lines by far are a close yellow-orange pair near 589 nm, the color of old streetlights. Neon’s many red and orange lines are the glow of a neon sign.
Melt the table. Color by State at a temperature and slide from 0 K up. Mercury and bromine are the liquids at room temperature; near 30 °C cesium and gallium join them (gallium melts in your hand); tungsten, the metal with the highest melting point, stays solid until 3,695 K.
Hear a family. In Element sounds, choose Electron melody and sweep a group: each element’s last notes climb one step as you go down, because each row adds a shell.

How it works

The orbitals. For each occupied subshell the page computes Slater’s effective charge, then draws points from the hydrogen-like |ψ|² for every occupied orbital: the distance from the radial probability r²R², the direction from |Ylm|² by accepting or rejecting random directions. Each electron gets the same number of points, so a pair of electrons makes a denser cloud. The dots are drawn with the browser’s WebGL, or on a plain canvas if WebGL isn’t available.

The nucleus uses the most common isotope in nature, or the longest-lived one for elements with none. Its protons and neutrons are packed as balls in a sphere whose volume grows with the number of particles, as a real nucleus does.

Colors of light. Each spectral line is colored from the CIE 1931 color-matching functions (in the fit by Wyman, Sloan and Shirley) converted to screen colors. Pure spectral colors are more vivid than any screen can show, so these are the nearest a screen can do, dimmed toward the ends of the visible range where the eye is less sensitive.

The sounds use the site’s own small synthesizer, with nothing recorded. A line at 656.3 nm (hydrogen’s red line) has a frequency of 456.7 trillion hertz; divided by 240 that is 415.3 Hz, close to the G♯ above middle C.
