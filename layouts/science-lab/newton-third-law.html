Newton’s Laws · Chapter 3

{{ .Title }}

Push on a wall and the wall pushes back on you, exactly as hard. That is not a figure of speech: it is how you walk, swim, row and fly. Every force is one half of a pair, and the two halves act on different things. Here two skaters push off each other, a horse pulls a cart while the cart pulls back on the horse, and a balloon flies by shoving air the other way. Along the way you will untangle a puzzle that has confused people since Newton’s time: if every pull is matched by an equal pull back, how does anything ever get going?

    The momentum bars. Momentum is mass × velocity. The skaters start with none. Afterward, A has some going left and B the same amount going right, so the green total stays at zero.

    The third law

    Whenever one object pushes or pulls on a second object, the second pushes or pulls back on the first, just as hard, in the opposite direction, at the same moment.

    FA on B = −FB on Athe force A exerts on B is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force B exerts on A

    The old names “action” and “reaction” make it sound as if one comes first and the other answers. It doesn’t. The two forces are two sides of one interaction. Neither skater can push without being pushed; you cannot touch anything without it touching you back.

    How to spot a third-law pair

    Two objects

    The two forces act on different objects: one on A, one on B. That is why they never cancel each other out.

    Swap the names

    If one is “A pushes B”, the other is “B pushes A”. Same two objects, roles swapped.

    Same kind

    Both are contact pushes, or both friction, or both gravity. A gravity force never pairs with a contact force.

    Same size, same time

    Equal in size, opposite in direction, and they start and stop together.

    Worked example: equal pushes, unequal speeds

    A 40 kg skater and an 80 kg skater push off each other with a steady 120 N for 0.5 s. Each gets the same push, 120 N × 0.5 s = 60 N·s, in opposite directions. By the second law, the change in each one’s velocity is that push divided by the mass:

    vA = 60 N·s ÷ 40 kg = 1.5 m/s left    vB = 60 N·s ÷ 80 kg = 0.75 m/s right

    Same force, twice the mass, half the speed. And look at the momenta: 40 × 1.5 = 60 kg·m/s left and 80 × 0.75 = 60 kg·m/s right. They add to zero, the same total the skaters started with. That is no accident: equal and opposite forces for equal times always leave the total momentum unchanged. Chapter 5 builds on exactly this.

    Try this: the simulation’s push rises and falls like a real one, so its impulse is 2 × peak force × time ÷ π. With 200 N for 0.5 s that is 63.7 N·s: 1.59 m/s for 40 kg and 0.80 m/s for 80 kg. Turn B into the wall: the wall pushes A just as hard as A pushes it, and A leaves at 63.7 ÷ 40 = 1.59 m/s, the same speed as before. The wall, firmly attached to the building and the Earth, does not visibly move.

    The horse-and-cart puzzle

    A horse pulls a cart. By the third law, the cart pulls back on the horse exactly as hard. So the forces cancel, and nothing should move. But horses do pull carts. What is wrong?

    The mistake is adding forces that act on different things. To find out whether the horse speeds up, add only the forces on the horse. To find out about the cart, add only the forces on the cart. Use the slider and watch the two diagrams below the picture.

    The horse and the cart

    The cart

    The answer. On the horse, two horizontal forces act: the ground pushes its hooves forward (that is the partner of the hooves pushing the ground backward), and the rope pulls it back. On the cart, the rope pulls it forward and the road holds it back. The horse speeds up when the ground pushes it forward harder than the rope pulls it back. The cart speeds up when the rope pulls it harder than the road holds it back. The rope’s pull on the horse and on the cart are always equal, and they never cancel, because they act on different bodies.

    So what really moves the horse and cart forward? The ground. The horse pushes backward on the Earth, and the Earth pushes forward on the horse. (The Earth gets pushed backward too, but with a mass of 6 × 1024 kg its change in motion is far too small to notice.)

    Worked example: a loaded cart

    A 600 kg horse pulls a 600 kg loaded cart. The road holds the cart back with 0.05 of its weight: 0.05 × 600 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 294 N. The horse pushes back on the ground with 1,000 N, so the ground pushes it forward with 1,000 N. Taking horse and cart together, the rope’s pulls are inside the system and cancel, so

    a = (1,000 N − 294 N) ÷ (600 kg + 600 kg) = 0.59 m/s²

    Now the cart alone: the rope must give it 600 × 0.59 = 353 N of net force on top of the 294 N of road friction, so the rope pulls with 647 N. And the horse alone: 1,000 N forward minus 647 N back is 353 N, and 353 ÷ 600 = 0.59 m/s². Both sides agree. Set the slider to 1,000 N with the loaded cart and read the same numbers in the panel.

    Common mistake

    “A book’s weight and the table’s upward push on it are an action–reaction pair.” They are equal and opposite, but they are not a pair. Both act on the same object, the book, and they are different kinds of force: one is gravity, the other a contact push. They are equal because the book is not accelerating (the first law), and they stop being equal if you put the book in an elevator that is speeding up. The real partners: the Earth pulls the book down, so the book pulls the Earth up, just as hard. The table pushes the book up, so the book pushes the table down.

    Newton’s own examples

    Lex III. Actioni contrariam semper & æqualem esse reactionem: sive corporum duorum actiones in se mutuo semper esse æquales & in partes contrarias dirigi.

    Law III. To every Action there is always opposed an equal Reaction: or the mutual actions of two bodies upon each other are always equal, and directed to contrary parts.

    Isaac Newton, Principia (London, 1687), Axioms, or Laws of Motion. English by Andrew Motte (London, 1729).

    Newton followed the law with everyday examples. A stone and a horse, more than three hundred years before this page:

    Quicquid premit vel trahit alterum, tantundem ab eo premitur vel trahitur. Siquis lapidem digito premit, premitur & hujus digitus a lapide. Si equus lapidem funi allegatum trahit, retrahetur etiam & equus æqualiter in lapidem: nam funis utrinque distentus eodem relaxandi se conatu urgebit equum versus lapidem, ac lapidem versus equum, tantumque impediet progressum unius quantum promovet progressum alterius.

    Whatever draws or presses another is as much drawn or pressed by that other. If you press a stone with your finger, the finger is also pressed by the stone. If a horse draws a stone tied to a rope, the horse (if I may so say) will be equally drawn back towards the stone: for the distended rope, by the same endeavour to relax or unbend itself, will draw the horse as much towards the stone as it does the stone towards the horse and will obstruct the progress of the one as much as it advances that of the other.

    Newton, Principia, after Law III. The Latin is from the first edition (1687); Motte translated the third edition (1726), which differs in small ways, such as “if I may so say”.

    Notice the last line: the rope “will obstruct the progress of the one as much as it advances that of the other.” Newton is saying exactly what the free-body diagrams show. The rope helps the stone (or cart) as much as it holds back the horse. Whether the horse gets anywhere depends on the ground.

    Pushing off: walking, swimming, flying

    You cannot push yourself forward. To speed up, you have to push something else backward, and let it push you. Try these.

    Walking. Each step, your foot pushes the floor backward, and friction from the floor pushes you forward. On a wood floor, shoes can get a push of up to 0.9 times your weight. On ice, only about 0.1 times your weight, so if you try to start quickly, your foot slips backward instead. That is why you take tiny steps on ice.

    Swimming and rowing. A swimmer pushes water backward with hands and feet, and the water pushes the swimmer forward. A swimmer pushing off the pool wall uses the wall instead: the wall pushes back on the swimmer.

    Rockets and jets. A rocket engine pushes hot gas out of its nozzle at great speed, and the gas pushes the rocket the other way. A rocket does not push on the air behind it; it works even better in the vacuum of space. A jet engine does the same with air it sucks in and blasts out. Design your own in Rocket Builder.

    Recoil. Throw a heavy ball from a skateboard, or step off a small boat onto a dock, and you move backward. The ball, or the boat, pushed back on you as hard as you pushed it.

    Where you see it

    • Cars move because the tires push backward on the road and the road pushes forward on the tires. On ice, the road can’t push back much, so wheels spin. Brakes work the same way in reverse: see the Brakes lesson. Tesla’s 2026 Roadster adds cold-gas thrusters that push air backward instead of pushing on the road: see it in a car in Thrusters and Fans.
    • A trebuchet flings its stone forward, and the stone pulls back on the sling and the arm just as hard. Try the Trebuchet Workshop.
    • Orbits: the Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon pulls the Earth just as hard. Both circle a point between them. See Orbit Lab and Chapter 6.
    • Leaning on a wall, sitting on a chair: you push down or sideways, and the wall or chair pushes back with exactly the force that keeps you in place.
    • Birds and helicopters push air downward with their wings or blades, and the air pushes them up.

    How the simulation works

    The skaters. During a push lasting T, each skater feels the same force, F(t) = Fpeak sin(πt ÷ T), A to the left and B to the right. In each step of 1 ms the simulation adds the exact integral of that force, the impulse, to each skater’s momentum, and moves them. With ice friction on, each moving skater also slows by μk g = 0.02 × 9.81 = 0.196 m/s² every second. The rink is 60 m long; a skater who reaches the boards stops there. The push plays at half speed so you can see it.

    Checks. For four pairs of masses (40 and 80 kg, 60 and 60, 30 and 90, 25 and 100), the total momentum stayed zero to better than 2 × 10−13 kg·m/s at every step, the impulse matched 2FT ÷ π to 10 decimal places, and the speed ratios came out 2, 1, 3 and 4 exactly. In every step the push given to A equaled the push given to B. After the push, the skaters coasted at constant speed. With friction, each skater’s final glide matched v² ÷ (2 μk g) to a tenth of a millimeter.

    The horse and cart use a = (Fground − f) ÷ (mhorse + mcart) and rope tension = mcart a + f, checked by confirming that each body separately obeys Fnet = ma. The horse is 600 kg and the road holds the cart back with 0.05 of its weight; both are round numbers picked for the model, and a real horse, cart and road could be quite different. If the horse doesn’t push harder than that, nothing moves and the rope simply carries the horse’s push. At 5 m/s the horse eases off to a steady trot. The demos use OpenStax’s friction values for shoes on wood (μs = 0.9) and on ice (μs = 0.1, μk = 0.05). The balloon is a 10 g model with a thrust falling from 0.3 N to zero in 1.2 s, and air drag; without drag, the balloon’s forward momentum and the air’s backward momentum summed to zero to 12 decimal places.

    Check yourself

    1. A 30 kg child and a 90 kg adult on skates push off each other. During the push, the force on the child is…

      Show the answer

      The two forces are a third-law pair: always equal in size, opposite in direction.

    2. After that push, the child glides away…

      Show the answer

      The same push on a third of the mass gives three times the change in velocity (the second law).

    3. A book rests on a table. The Earth pulls the book down. What is the third-law partner of that force?

      Show the answer

      Swap the names: “Earth pulls book” pairs with “book pulls Earth”, both gravity. The table’s push acts on the book too, so it cannot be its partner.

    4. A horse pulls a cart and they speed up together. What pushes the horse forward?

      Show the answer

      The rope pulls the horse and the cart equally hard. The horse speeds up because the ground pushes its hooves forward more than the rope pulls it back.

    5. Why is it hard to start walking quickly on smooth ice?

      Show the answer

      To speed up, the ground must push you forward, and on ice friction limits that push to about a tenth of your weight.

    6. How does a rocket speed up in empty space, where there is no air?

      Show the answer

      The rocket and its exhaust push on each other. Air is not needed; in fact, it only gets in the way.
