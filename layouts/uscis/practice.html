How the civics test works

The civics test is part of the naturalization interview. The officer reads questions from the official list, and you answer out loud. Any answer on the list counts. USCIS says there may be other correct answers, but it encourages applicants to use the ones on the list. For questions such as “Name two…,” give that many answers.

Some answers change with elections and appointments: the names of the President, the Vice President, the Speaker of the House and the Chief Justice, and on the 2008 list the number of Supreme Court justices and the President’s party. Give the answer that is true on the day of your interview; USCIS keeps the current answers at uscis.gov/citizenship/testupdates. Other answers depend on where you live, such as your governor, your state capital, your U.S. senators and your U.S. representative. This page leaves all of these names out, because they change.

If you do not pass the civics test (or the English test) at the interview, USCIS gives you a second chance on the part you missed, usually 60 to 90 days later. Some applicants qualify for exceptions; USCIS explains them on its site.

The versions over time

  • October 1, 2008. A redesigned naturalization test took effect with a list of 100 civics questions. The officer asks up to 10, and 6 correct passes.
  • December 1, 2020. USCIS began a revised civics test with 128 questions, 20 asked and 12 correct to pass.
  • March 1, 2021. USCIS went back to the 2008 test. Applicants who had filed between December 1, 2020, and March 1, 2021, could choose either test if their first interview came before April 19, 2021.
  • October 20, 2025. The 2025 version, based on the 2020 test with changes, applies to applications filed on or after this date: 128 questions, up to 20 asked, 12 correct to pass. USCIS announced it on September 17, 2025. Applicants who filed before October 20, 2025, take the 2008 version.

People disagree about how hard the test should be. Some say a longer test better shows real knowledge of American government and history; others say the longer test adds a burden without telling much more about an applicant. This page takes no side; it helps you practice the version you will take.

Sources and how the lists were checked

The questions and answers are works of the U.S. government, which are in the public domain, and they appear here word for word, with the official numbering, section headings, asterisks and words in parentheses. Each list was transcribed from two separate copies, compared line by line by a script, and every difference was settled with a third look (transcribed {{ (index $U "2025").transcribed }}). On {{ (index $U "2025").rechecked }} both lists were checked again, question by question, against a copy of the official 2025 PDF (M-1778 (09/25)) and current copies of the 2008 list; the one change was adding “Juneteenth” to question 100 of the 2008 version (see the differences below).
