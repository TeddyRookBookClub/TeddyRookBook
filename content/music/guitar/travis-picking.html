This style is named for Merle Travis, a Kentucky guitarist who made it famous in the 1940s, though players in his part of Kentucky (and blues and ragtime guitarists before them) were already using it. The rule is simple: the thumb plays every beat, alternating between two bass strings, and it never waits for the fingers.

Step 1: the thumb alone

Hold a C chord. The thumb plays string 5 on beat 1 and string 4 on beat 2, then string 6 (move finger 3 down to its 3rd fret, G) on beat 3 and string 4 again on beat 4. Say “one, two, three, four” and keep it steady for a minute.

Step 2: fingers between

Now add the middle finger on string 1 and the index on string 2, on the “and”s. The basic Travis pattern:

The letters under the strings show who plays: the thumb (T) on the bass, the middle finger (M) on string 1, the index (I) on string 2.

Step 3: pinches and a tune

When a finger note falls on the same beat as the thumb, pluck them together (a pinch). Choosing which top notes to play, and when, turns the pattern into a tune. That’s what Whistle Stop Rag does, over the ragtime chord circle C, A7, D7, G7.

Cstring 5, 4, 6 (3rd fret), 4
A7string 5, 4, 5, 4
D7string 4, 3, 4, 3
G7string 6, 4, 6, 4

Whistle Stop Rag

Try it. Thumb alone on C for one minute, then on G7, then switching every bar. Add the fingers only when the thumb runs without thinking. Then learn Whistle Stop Rag at 60%, bar by bar.

Remember

  • The thumb alternates two bass strings on every beat, and never waits.
  • Fingers pick between the beats; a pinch is finger and thumb together.
  • Learn the thumb first, the fingers second.
