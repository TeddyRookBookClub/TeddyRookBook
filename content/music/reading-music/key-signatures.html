Right after the clef, at the start of every line, you may see a few sharps or flats. That’s the key signature. It means: play these notes sharp (or flat) everywhere, in every octave, for the whole piece, unless a natural sign says otherwise.

Always in the same order

Sharps and flats in a key signature always appear in a fixed order, so a key signature with two sharps always has the same two.

Sharps: F C G D A E B

One sharp is always F♯. Two sharps are F♯ and C♯, and so on. A saying to help: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle.

Flats: B E A D G C F

The same letters backward. One flat is always B♭; two flats are B♭ and E♭. The first four spell BEAD, or say Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles’ Father.

Naming the key

A key signature tells you the key of the music: the home note that it settles on. Two quick rules for major keys:

Sharps

Look at the last sharp (the one furthest right). The key is a half step higher. Last sharp C♯, one half step up is D: D major.

Flats

The second-to-last flat names the key. B♭ E♭ A♭: the second-to-last is E♭, so E♭ major. Just one flat? That’s F major; learn that one by heart.

No sharps or flats at all means C major.

Major and minor share signatures

Every key signature belongs to two keys: a major key and its relative minor, which starts three half steps lower. One sharp is G major or E minor; no sharps or flats is C major or A minor. To tell which, look at the music itself: a piece in E minor usually leans on E and often ends on it.

Remember

  • The key signature applies to every note with that letter, in every octave, all piece long.
  • Order of sharps: F C G D A E B. Order of flats: B E A D G C F.
  • Sharps: last sharp + half step = the key. Flats: second-to-last flat = the key. One flat = F.
  • Each signature also fits a minor key, three half steps lower.
