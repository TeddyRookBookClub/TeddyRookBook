At a jam, players take turns: one person sings or plays the melody (a break), and everyone else plays backup. Here are the main ways a banjo backs others up.

Vamping (bluegrass)

Hold a moveable chord shape (no open strings), pick it with a short T+I+M pinch on beats 2 and 4, and loosen your fretting hand right after each pinch so the chord stops short: a “chop.” It sounds like a snare drum and leaves room for the singer. Here is G with the F shape at the 5th fret, then C with the F shape at the 10th, D at the 12th and back to G:

The shapes are the top three strings of the F shape from the More Chords chapter. On the beats with rests, your hand is still moving in time; you just don’t play.

Rolls as backup

Behind a fiddle or a slow song, play quiet forward or forward-reverse rolls on the chords, with no melody: a soft, ringing carpet under the tune. Play it much softer than you would play a break, and leave space when the singer sings.

Clawhammer backup

An old-time jam has no solos: everybody plays the tune together, round and round. The clawhammer banjo plays the melody with the fiddle, simplified as much as you need. When you don’t know a tune yet, play bum-ditties on the chords and listen; the melody will come.

How a jam works

  • Tune up first, quietly, with a clip-on tuner.
  • Listen more than you play. Play softly enough to hear the melody.
  • Watch the leader. At a bluegrass jam, the person who started the tune nods to whoever takes the next break. It’s fine to shake your head and pass.
  • Learn the jam favorites: Boil Them Cabbage Down, Soldier’s Joy, Arkansas Traveler and the other tunes on our songs page come up again and again.
  • Keep going. If you lose your place, stop playing, listen for the start of the next part and come back in. Nobody minds.

A practice plan

Warm up (5 minutes)

Slow rolls or bum-ditties on G, C and D7. Relaxed hands, even notes.

New work (10–15 minutes)

One new tune or technique, slowly, with the loop and the metronome. Two bars at a time.

Play for fun (5–10 minutes)

Tunes you already know, at a comfortable tempo, all the way through without stopping.

Twenty or thirty minutes a day does more than two hours once a week. Stop while it’s still fun, rest when your hands are tired, and stop if anything hurts.

Try it. Play the vamping exercise along with the metronome clicking on beats 2 and 4. Then put on any recording of a bluegrass or old-time band in G and play backup along with it, rolls or bum-ditties, changing chords by ear.

Check each one when you’ve tried it.

  • I can vamp on beats 2 and 4 with the metronome.
  • I can play rolls or bum-ditty backup while changing chords by ear.
  • I followed the three-part practice plan for a week.

Remember

  • Vamping: closed chord shapes, short chops on beats 2 and 4.
  • Rolls make soft backup; clawhammer players play the tune along with the fiddle.
  • At a jam: tune up, listen, play softly, watch the leader, keep going.
