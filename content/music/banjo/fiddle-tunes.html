For generations, fiddle and banjo have played together for Southern dances. The tunes they play share a simple, practical shape.

AABB

A typical fiddle tune has two parts, the A part and the B part, usually eight bars each. The band plays A twice, then B twice, and then the whole thing again, as long as the dancers want. Often the A part is lower and the B part higher. In the music, repeat signs (a thick line with two dots) mark each part; our player follows them, and the loop lets you practice one part at a time.

When you play alone, pick the key that is easiest. When you play with a fiddler, play in the fiddler’s key: that’s what the capo and the other tunings are for.

Learning a tune by ear

  • Listen to the tune many times before you play it, until you can hum the A part and the B part.
  • Find the first few notes on the banjo (the scale chapter helps). Most tunes start and end on the key note.
  • Learn one phrase at a time (two bars), then join phrases.
  • Keep it simple at first: the notes on the beats are enough. Fill in the rest later.

Arkansas Traveler, three-finger

The A part uses the alternating-thumb roll with the melody on the thumb. The B part runs down the scale with pull-offs. In bar 7 the open 5th string plays the high G of the melody.

Try it. Learn the A part of Arkansas Traveler until you can play it twice through without stopping. Then the B part. Then play AABB at a slow, even tempo: steady is more important than fast at a dance. Afterwards, try the clawhammer version of the same tune and compare.

Check each one as you go.

  • I can say what AABB means and play a tune in that order.
  • I learned one phrase of a tune by ear before looking at the tab.
  • I can play Arkansas Traveler (or its clawhammer version) all the way through.

Remember

  • Fiddle tunes are AABB: two eight-bar parts, each played twice.
  • Play in the fiddler’s key: retune or use the capo.
  • Learn by ear in short phrases; the notes on the beat come first.
