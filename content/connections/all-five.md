### A wave that has to fit

Tie a string at both ends and pluck it. Only certain waves can live on it: the ones that fit a whole number of half-waves between the ends. One half-wave, two, three. Nothing in between.

That one fact has five faces.

- **Music.** The fitting waves are the harmonics, at 1, 2, 3 times the lowest frequency. They are why a string has a pitch and why some notes sound good together.
- **Math.** The whole numbers are forced by the equation. The same equation, written by Jean d'Alembert in 1747, covers every case below.
- **Patterns.** A fitting wave has places that never move. On a string they are points. On a plate they are the lines where sand collects.
- **Chemistry.** An electron in an atom is a standing wave too. Its fitting patterns are the orbitals, and counting them gives the shape of the periodic table.
- **Physics.** In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has a wavelength. Ask the electron's wave to fit a whole number of times round its orbit and you get Bohr's rule, and with it the energy levels.

### Why the numbers are whole

Nothing in nature picks out whole numbers on purpose. They appear whenever a wave is shut in: by the ends of a string, the edge of a drum, the pull of a nucleus, or the need to join up with itself round a circle. Shutting a wave in turns a smooth world into a counted one.

### Where it stops

The likeness is real but it has limits, and the regions around this one mark them. Hydrogen's lines are not a musical scale. The planets do not sing. A pattern counts here only when it comes with its reason.
