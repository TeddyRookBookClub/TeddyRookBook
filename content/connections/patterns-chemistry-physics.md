### Layers that reflect

The atoms of a crystal sit in flat, evenly spaced layers. X-rays have wavelengths about as long as the gap between layers. In 1912 Max von Laue's group sent X-rays through a crystal and found a pattern of sharp spots on the film behind it. The crystal was acting like the slits in the Patterns and Physics region, in three dimensions.

Within a year William Lawrence Bragg, then in his early twenties, and his father William Henry Bragg found the simple rule. X-rays reflected from one layer and from the layer below travel different distances. They come out in step, and make a bright spot, only when

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda,$$

where $d$ is the gap between layers, $\theta$ the angle of the beam, $\lambda$ the wavelength and $n$ a whole number.

[[viz:ph-bragg|X-rays reflecting from two layers of atoms. Change the angle. The reflected waves come out in step, and the detector lights up, only at the angles Bragg's law allows.]]

### What it gave chemistry

Measure the angles and you know the spacing of the atoms. The Braggs worked out table salt and diamond in the first year. Later the same method gave the structures of proteins and, in 1953, of DNA.

### A pattern that was not supposed to exist

A repeating crystal can have two-fold, three-fold, four-fold or six-fold symmetry, and never five-fold. In 1982 Dan Shechtman saw a sharp spot pattern with ten-fold symmetry. The material was ordered but did not repeat: a quasicrystal, the real-world cousin of the Penrose tilings in the Math and Patterns region. He received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011.
