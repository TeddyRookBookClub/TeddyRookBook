### An atom is a tiny drum

Hit a drum and its skin vibrates in a mix of patterns, or **modes**. Each mode is a standing wave with its own still lines (nodes), its own symmetry and its own frequency. Unlike a string, a drum's modes are *not* whole-number multiples of each other: their frequencies follow the zeros of special functions called Bessel functions, in ratios like 1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30. That is why most drums sound like a thump rather than a clear note.

An electron in an atom is a standing wave too, but in three dimensions. Its shapes, the **orbitals**, come in families by symmetry: s orbitals are round with no node through the middle, p orbitals have one node plane, d orbitals two, f orbitals three, just like drum modes with 0, 1, 2 and 3 node lines.

### How those shapes build the periodic table

Each orbital holds at most two electrons, and each shape comes in $2l + 1$ versions: 1 s orbital, 3 p, 5 d, 7 f. So the s, p, d and f families hold 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons. Electrons fill them in a set order (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d …), and that order **is** the shape of the periodic table: two columns for s, six for p, ten for d and fourteen for f. Rows come out 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements long.

So these four subjects meet here. Music gives us standing waves and their frequencies. Math describes them exactly. Their symmetry makes the patterns. And chemistry is what you get when nature fills those patterns with electrons.
