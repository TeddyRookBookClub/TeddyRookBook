### A string made of beads

Hang a row of equal beads on springs and pluck it. It has standing waves, just as a guitar string does. But a row of $N$ beads has exactly $N$ of them, and no more. The shortest possible wave has neighboring beads moving in opposite directions, and nothing can wiggle faster than that.

A crystal is the same thing in three dimensions: atoms in a repeating pattern, held in place by chemical bonds that act like springs.

[[viz:ph-chain|A row of atoms joined by springs. Choose one of its standing waves and hear its pitch. The pitches rise more and more slowly and then stop: a row of atoms has a highest note.]]

### Sound and heat

A sound wave passing through a metal bar is a long, slow version of these waves. It moves fast, about 5,000 meters a second in steel against 343 in air, because the bonds are stiff. Heat in a solid is the same motion at very short wavelengths: all the allowed waves ringing at once, at random.

### Why a bell rings

Strike a bell and you set off its own standing waves. The bronze of a bell, about four parts copper to one part tin, is hard and loses very little energy to friction inside the metal, so the ring lasts. The pattern of the atoms sets the stiffness, the stiffness sets the speed of sound, and the speed and the bell's shape set the note.
