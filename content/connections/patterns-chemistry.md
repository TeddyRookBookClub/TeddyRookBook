### Seven shapes of a crystal

A crystal is atoms stacked in a pattern that repeats in three directions, like wallpaper in 3D. The smallest box that repeats is the **unit cell**. Its shape is set by three edge lengths, $a$, $b$ and $c$, and the three angles between them.

Sorted by the symmetry they allow, all crystals fall into **seven crystal systems**. Add the ways atoms can sit inside the box, with every turn, mirror and screw, and you get exactly **230 space groups**. Fedorov and Arthur Schoenflies each finished the count in 1891.

**Snowflakes.** Ice is hexagonal: its water molecules link into rings of six. As a snow crystal grows, its six corners stick out into moist air and grow fastest, so it sprouts six arms. Each arm feels nearly the same air, which is why the arms look alike. Johannes Kepler wondered about the six-fold shape in a little book in 1611, long before anyone knew about molecules.

**Quasicrystals.** In 1982 Dan Shechtman shone electrons through an aluminum–manganese alloy and saw a pattern with ten-fold symmetry, which ordinary crystals can't have. Many scientists doubted him for years. The pattern is ordered but never repeats, like a Penrose tiling. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011.

### The chemistry of stained glass

Plain glass is mostly melted sand. Medieval glassmakers colored the whole pot by stirring in metal compounds, so it was called **pot-metal** glass. Two kinds of chemistry make the colors:

- **Metal ions** dissolved in the glass soak up some colors of light and let the rest through. Cobalt gives blue; copper in an airy fire gives blue-green; manganese gives purple; iron gives green and yellow.
- **Tiny specks of metal**, far too small to see, make other colors. Copper specks give red, gold specks give rose-red, and silver gives yellow.

> **Myth vs fact.** Old windows are often thicker at the bottom, and people say the glass has "flowed" over the centuries. It hasn't: at room temperature glass would need far longer than the age of the universe to sag that much. Hand-made panes were uneven, and glaziers usually set the thick edge at the bottom.
