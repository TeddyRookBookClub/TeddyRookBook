### Sand on the still lines, electrons off them

On a ringing plate, sand collects on the **nodes**, the lines and circles that never move. An electron in an atom is also described by a standing wave, found by Erwin Schrödinger's equation in 1926. Where that wave is zero, there is a node too, and the electron is never found there. So the two pictures are opposites in one way: the sand marks the nodes, while the electron's dots avoid them. But the patterns of nodes are close cousins.

An orbital is labeled by two numbers we need here: $n$ (the shell) and $l$ (the shape: $l = 0, 1, 2$ for s, p, d). It has $l$ flat nodes (planes through the nucleus) and $n - l - 1$ round nodes (spheres). A round plate's modes are counted the same way: some lines through the center and some circles. Line up the counts and the two pictures match.

### Where the analogy stops

A plate is flat and an atom is three-dimensional, so the orbital's nodes are planes, cones and spheres, not lines. The sizes don't match either: the orbital's round nodes aren't spaced like the plate's circles. And an orbital is not a picture of a moving electron; it shows where the electron is likely to be found. What carries over is the deep idea: a wave trapped in a space can only settle into certain patterns, and each pattern has its own frequency, or for an atom, its own energy.
