### One force law, two sizes

Gravity pulls a planet toward the Sun with a force that weakens as the square of the distance. The electric force pulls an electron toward a proton in the same way. So in Bohr's model the electron obeys Kepler's third law, just as a planet does: the square of the orbit's time goes as the cube of its size. Only the constant changes, by a factor of about 5 × 10¹⁷.

### Bohr's rule

A planet can orbit at any distance. Bohr added one rule that a planet does not have: the electron's angular momentum must be a whole number $n$ times a fixed amount. That allows only certain orbits, with sizes that grow as $n^2$ and energies

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}.$$

When the electron drops from a higher orbit to a lower one, the atom gives off light whose color carries exactly the energy lost.

### What survived

The real electron is a standing wave, not a tiny planet, and Bohr's circles were replaced by quantum mechanics twelve years later. But his energies are exactly right for hydrogen, and his rule explains the whole-number formula that Balmer found in the Music, Math and Chemistry region.
