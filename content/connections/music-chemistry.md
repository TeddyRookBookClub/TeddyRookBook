### Newlands' law of octaves

By the 1860s chemists knew about sixty elements and their atomic weights, but no one had a good way to arrange them. In 1865 the English chemist John Newlands listed the elements in order of atomic weight and noticed that similar ones turned up at regular intervals. Lithium, sodium and potassium, all soft metals that fizz in water, came seven places apart, and so did fluorine and chlorine. Counting lithium as the first, sodium is the eighth.

Newlands compared this to music: the eighth element from any starting point repeats the first, just as the eighth note of a scale repeats the first one an octave higher. Count lithium as *do*, and sodium lands on the next *do*. He called it the **law of octaves**.

When he presented it to the Chemical Society of London on March 1, 1866, the idea was mocked. One listener asked whether he had tried arranging the elements alphabetically, and the society declined to publish his paper. The pattern did break down after calcium, where heavier elements refused to fit.

Three years later Dmitri Mendeleev published a periodic table that left gaps for elements not yet found, and it won the day. But the Royal Society later gave Newlands its **Davy Medal** (1887) for discovering the periodic law. Today we know why his octaves stopped: the noble gases (found in the 1890s) add one more element to each early row, and from the fourth row on the transition metals stretch the rows to 18.

> **Myth vs fact.** You may read that "each element has its own musical note." Newlands' octave was a way of counting, not a sound: elements don't hum. What is real is that atoms and molecules have exact frequencies at which they absorb and give off light. Those are a hundred billion times higher or more than any note you can hear, so any "element music" is an artist's choice of how to scale them down.

### Molecules vibrate: their "notes"

The atoms in a molecule are held together by bonds that act a bit like springs. A molecule can stretch, squeeze and bend, and like a guitar string, each way of moving has its own natural frequency. A light atom on a stiff bond vibrates fast; heavy atoms on a loose bond vibrate slowly:

$$f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}},$$

where $k$ is the stiffness of the bond and $\mu$ is a kind of average mass of the two atoms.

A water molecule has three ways to vibrate. Shine infrared light on it and it soaks up exactly the frequencies that match those vibrations, much as a wine glass rings when a singer hits its note. Chemists measure these frequencies as **wavenumbers** (waves per centimeter of light); an infrared spectrum works like a molecule's fingerprint.
