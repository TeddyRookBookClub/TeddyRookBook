## How it works

Each work is cut into daily passages that always end at the end of a sentence. Read the passage, write your English in the box under it, then press **Check your translation** to compare it with a public-domain translation. Your work stays in this browser. You can print a day or save all your translations as a text file.

Want it in your calendar? The [New Year’s resolution builder](/life-planner/resolution/) has *Translate a Latin classic* and *Translate a Greek classic* goals. It shares the work out over the days you choose and links each day straight to its lines. You can also add any of these as a track in the [Life Planner](/life-planner/).

## Be honest with yourself about grammar

Translating the *Aeneid* in a year is a big goal. It is about 27 lines every day. For someone who has finished a first-year Latin course, that is often close to an hour a day, and slower in the first months. Virgil and Homer write poetry, with free word order and old forms, so they are hard places to start.

If you are new to Latin or Greek, spend this year on grammar and an easier text, and save the *Aeneid* or the *Iliad* for next year. The [old Harvard list](/languages/translate/harvard/) page has a suggested order. Good places to begin on this site:

- **Latin:** [D’Ooge’s *Latin for Beginners*](/homeschool/latin/), with a step-by-step guide for each of its 79 lessons. Then Caesar.
- **Greek:** the [Greek Alphabet](/languages/alphabet/) lessons, then [Machen’s *New Testament Greek for Beginners*](/homeschool/greek/) and the [Greek and Latin with Jerome](/languages/jerome/) study companion. They lead straight into the Gospel of John. Xenophon and Homer need more grammar after that. *Smyth’s Greek Grammar* (1920) is the standard reference, [free online](https://ccel.org/s/smyth/grammar/html/toc_uni.htm).

## Are the original texts in the public domain?

Yes. The ancient works themselves have no copyright. A modern *edition* can be copyrighted, because an editor chooses between the manuscripts and writes the notes. So every text here comes from an edition printed long enough ago to be in the public domain in the United States, and every English translation too:

- **Virgil:** J. B. Greenough’s text (Ginn, 1881). English: Theodore C. Williams (1910).
- **Caesar:** T. Rice Holmes’s Oxford text (1914). English: McDevitte and Bohn (1869).
- **Cicero:** A. C. Clark’s Oxford text (1905, 1908 printing). English: C. D. Yonge (1856).
- **Xenophon:** E. C. Marchant’s Oxford text (1904). English: Carleton L. Brownson (Loeb, 1922).
- **Homer:** Monro and Allen’s Oxford text (3rd edition, 1920). English: A. T. Murray (Loeb, 1924–1925).
- **John:** Eberhard Nestle’s Greek New Testament (1904), the same text as the site’s [Gospel Reader](/languages/reader/). English: the King James Version.

The Latin, Greek and English come from the [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu) at Tufts University, which shares its digital editions under the [Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/) license. Our daily-portion files are built from them and shared under the same license. The text is shown as the editors printed it. Greenough’s Virgil and these Greek texts have no long-vowel marks (macrons), so none are added here. Square brackets [ ] mark words or lines the editor doubted were by the author, and angle brackets ⟨ ⟩ mark words the editor added where the manuscripts have a gap.
